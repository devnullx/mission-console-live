Tissue engineering / bioprocessing

Bioreactor for tissue engineering and biomanufacturing

Engineered tissues such as cartilage and heart muscle are hard to grow on Earth. Thick 3D constructs starve in the middle, and gravity makes cells and soft gels settle. In microgravity there is no sedimentation and no buoyant convection, so a perfusion bioreactor must deliver nutrients and oxygen by pumped flow alone. This lets researchers separate the effects of gravity, flow and shear on tissue growth. Flight results so far show that gravity matters: cartilage grown on Mir came back smaller and mechanically weaker, and engineered heart tissues on the ISS lost contractile force. That makes space a test bed for tissue-engineering methods, disease models and ideas for in-space biomanufacturing.

How LELP-1 runs it

Sample. Pre-formed 3D engineered tissue constructs, for example chondrocyte-seeded porous scaffold discs or iPSC-cardiomyocyte tissues. 2 constructs in each of 4 independent perfusion chambers (8 constructs per bay), all from one batch shared with the ground control. Container. Large-bay perfusion module: 4 sealed perfusion chambers on a closed-loop flow path, a medium reservoir, a waste bag, fixative, RNA-stabilizer and rinse reservoirs, a silicone membrane gas exchanger with a bubble trap, in-line pH, dissolved-oxygen and glucose/lactate sensors, a camera, and a small thermoelectric cold pad under each chamber plus an insulated pocket for medium aliquots, so fixed chambers can be held at 4 C inside the 37 C bay. Launch. Live constructs, matured on the ground, in HEPES-buffered medium at 37 C; late-loaded about 48 h or less before launch with the bay powered. Light and gas. Dark except short LED pulses for imaging. No CO2 supply, so the medium is HEPES-buffered. Oxygen reaches the medium from the sealed bay air through the membrane gas exchanger. pH and dissolved oxygen are logged continuously.

  1. day -2

    Load constructs into the 4 chambers, prime medium, seal bay, start 37 C hold and slow perfusion; late load into LELP-1.

  2. day 0

    Launch. Perfusion paused or at minimum flow during ascent; temperature, pressure and acceleration logged.

  3. day 1

    After orbit checkout: start nominal intermittent perfusion, take baseline images of every construct, log pH, dissolved oxygen, glucose and lactate.

  4. day 2

    Early time point: chamber A is valved off, drained and filled with 4% paraformaldehyde. After 24 h it is flushed with buffer and its cold pad cools it to 4 C.

  5. day 3

    Days 3-6: daily partial exchange of fresh medium; spent-medium aliquot to the cold pocket; imaging every 6 h (construct size and shape; beat rate for cardiac tissue).

  6. day 7

    Mid time point: chamber B fixed and cooled as for chamber A. Optional test compound added to chamber C; chamber D stays as the untreated control.

  7. day 8

    Days 8-12: continue feeding, sensing and imaging; robotic-arm moves scheduled outside imaging windows.

  8. day 13

    End point: chamber C fixed in paraformaldehyde and later flushed with buffer; chamber D filled with RNA stabilizer. Whole bay then set to 4 C.

  9. day 14

    Lab operations end; bay held at 4 C through deorbit, splashdown and courier hand-over.

Measured in orbit

  • Medium pH In-line optical pH sensor spot read by the module, continuous log
  • Dissolved oxygen In-line optical oxygen sensor spots upstream and downstream of the chambers; the difference gives oxygen consumption
  • Glucose and lactate In-line flow sensor on the return line; gives the metabolic rate of each chamber
  • Construct size, shape and beating Module camera (brightfield, plus fluorescence if reporters are used) every 6 h; video clips for beat rate in cardiac tissue
  • Environment Bay temperature, pressure, humidity and acceleration sensors; flags arm-motion periods
  • Radiation dose Bay active silicon dosimeter plus passive TLDs

Measured after the return

  • Histology and matrix staining Sections of fixed constructs: proteoglycan (Safranin-O) and type II collagen immunostaining for cartilage; sarcomere and cell structure staining for cardiac tissue
  • Matrix biochemistry Glycosaminoglycan, collagen (hydroxyproline) and DNA content per construct
  • Gene expression RNA sequencing of RNA-stabilized constructs, flight versus ground
  • Ultrastructure Electron microscopy of sarcomeres and mitochondria on fixed samples
  • Spent-medium analysis Cytokines, metabolites and matrix fragments in cold-stored aliquots
  • Dose and environment reconstruction Read TLDs; merge with in-flight dosimeter, temperature and acceleration logs

Ground control

An identical perfusion module in a ground bay with constructs from the same batch, run at 1 g on the same feeding, imaging and fixation timeline. It is offset by a few hours so it can replay the flight temperature log. Launch vibration and re-entry loads are replayed on a shaker where possible. An optional third group in a rotating-wall or clinostat setup separates flow and shear effects from gravity effects.

Coming home

Fixed and RNA-stabilized constructs and medium aliquots should stay at 4 C from the end of operations to the customer lab. About 20 min to the courier plus 1-2 days of transport fits within 72 h. Mechanical testing needs live, unfixed constructs. A live-return option would need the bay held near 37 C (or at a validated holding temperature) through recovery, which has not yet been demonstrated for LELP-1.

Limits of this flight

  • 14 days is short for tissue growth. The Mir cartilage ran 4 months after 3 months on Earth. LELP-1 suits early responses and method tests, not growing mature tissue.
  • Microgravity has not been shown to make better tissue. Mir cartilage was smaller and mechanically weaker, and ISS heart tissues lost contractile force. Customers should expect to study gravity effects, not to receive a better product.
  • No crew. On CRS-20 the crew exchanged medium by syringe; on LELP-1 every medium change and fixation must be automatic, and nobody can clear a blocked line. Gas bubbles do not rise in microgravity, so the flow path needs a bubble trap and fully automatic recovery.
  • No CO2 supply. The Mir BTS used 10% CO2 and the CRS-20 incubator used 5% CO2. HEPES-buffered medium changes metabolism compared with those setups, so the ground control must use the same medium.
  • Oxygen comes only from the sealed bay air, and the cells' CO2 builds up there. The gas budget must be sized for each customer, because cardiac tissue uses far more oxygen than cartilage.
  • Live tissue must wait about 2 days at the pad and survive ascent loads. Returned tissue is fixed or stabilized, so mechanical properties cannot be tested after return unless a live-return mode is developed.
  • Robotic-arm moves raise acceleration to about 1e-4 to 1e-3 g.
  • Volumes are millilitres and there are 8 constructs per bay: a research-scale study, not production biomanufacturing.
  • In-line glucose/lactate sensing and the per-chamber cold pads are our design choices and have not flown on LELP-1.

A satellite of its own: SP-BRX

If this experiment flies alone instead of in a LELP-1 bay, SP-BRX is a 79 kg free-flyer: a service module with a 0.40 m² sun-tracking array (120 W), a 241 Wh battery, S-band radios and a green-monopropellant deorbit module (2 × 22 N, a 340 s burn), carrying a 25 kg return capsule with a 0.87 m heat shield. It flies the same 550 km dawn-dusk orbit: two days of checkout, then 14 days of protocol, then deorbit; the capsule holds its samples at temperature for 8 h while the ship finds it.

Tailored for this experiment

  • Bay powered at 37 C during the pad wait and ascent, with late load no more than about 48 h before launch (live tissues)
  • Small 4 C cold pads and an insulated pocket inside the 37 C bay for fixed chambers and medium aliquots, since a bay holds only one setpoint
  • Image and sensor downlink on each ground pass so the ground team can adjust flow and feeding
  • Robotic-arm moves scheduled outside imaging and fixation windows to keep the bay near 1e-6 g
  • Optional: a small pre-charged 5% CO2 gas cartridge if a customer needs bicarbonate-buffered medium (not a standard bay service)

Bay or satellite?

On LELP-1 this payload takes one bay (5.5 kg, 2.3 % of the lab's launch mass), shares power, radios and the return with 31 others, and comes home with the whole lab. Alone, it needs 79 kg of satellite, about 14 times its own mass, but it gets the schedule to itself and a quieter ride: about 7.3 × 10⁻⁸ g at the payload, with no robotic arm, against about 7 × 10⁻⁷ g in a LELP-1 bay.

First-order sizing with a 20 % system margin by the same model for every experiment, reviewed independently; read the caveats. Flown recoverable free-flyers are heavier, so treat these masses as optimistic.
