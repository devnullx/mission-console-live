Immunology

Immune T-cell activation

Astronauts show immune dysregulation and reactivation of latent herpesviruses that last through 6-month ISS missions. On Earth, weak T-cell activation is also seen in older people. Since Spacelab 1 in 1983, flight experiments have found that human lymphocytes activated in suspension with the mitogen Con A respond much less in microgravity than on the ground. In a 2006 ISS study, gene expression already differed from the on-board 1 g control 1.5 hours after activation. Microgravity lets researchers weaken early T-cell activation without drugs or genetic edits, and study those early steps.

Who it is for

  • University and hospital immunology labs studying T-cell signalling and immunosenescence (ageing of the immune system)
  • Space agency human-research programmes that need data on astronaut immune dysregulation and on countermeasures
  • Pharmaceutical and biotech groups testing immunomodulating compounds on a model where T-cell activation is already impaired
  • Research groups that already have ISS T-cell data and want a repeat flight with a different activator, donor set or time points

How LELP-1 runs it

Sample. Human peripheral T cells (or PBMC, which keep the accessory cells) from 2 donors. Cells are cryopreserved before launch. They can be pre-labelled with a cell-tracking dye so proliferation can be measured after return. Container. Large bay 24-well cassette with gas-permeable lids and a filtered (about 0.4 um) outlet on each well, so supernatant can be drawn off without removing cells. Planned layout: 2 donors x 3 conditions (unstimulated; Con A + anti-CD28 to match the ISS heritage; soluble anti-CD3/anti-CD28) x 3 time points = 18 wells, plus 6 wells for imaging, viability and spares. Launch. cryopreserved (-80 C passive cassette); medium, activators and fixatives held in bay reservoirs. Light and gas. No light needed (dark incubation; the microscope LEDs run only during imaging). No CO2 supply: HEPES-buffered RPMI-type medium in sealed wells with gas-permeable lids.

  1. day -1

    Load the cryo cassette, reagent reservoirs (HEPES medium, Con A + anti-CD28, anti-CD3/anti-CD28, RNAlater, paraformaldehyde fixative) and the empty well cassette. Fix a pre-launch baseline aliquot of each donor on the ground.

  2. day 0

    Launch. Cells stay in the passive -80 C cassette. Bay reservoirs are held at 4 C.

  3. day 1

    Automated thaw at 37 C, then about 1:10 dilution into warm medium to lower the DMSO level, and dispensing into the wells. Brightfield images give the starting cell count and morphology.

  4. day 2

    After an 18-24 h rest at 37 C, fix one unstimulated set (t = 0) in RNAlater. Pump the activators into the stimulated wells. Lock out arm motion for the first 2 h. At 1.5 h, add RNAlater to the early-gene wells, as in the 2006 ISS flight.

  5. day 3

    24 h after activation: draw supernatant through the well filter into a sealed sample reservoir for cytokine analysis, then fix the cells with paraformaldehyde for flow cytometry (CD69, CD25). Take brightfield images of cell clustering every 6 h from day 2.

  6. day 5

    72 h after activation: split the final wells between paraformaldehyde (proliferation, Ki-67 or dye dilution) and RNAlater. Image all wells. Set the bay to 4 C.

  7. day 7

    Bay held at 4 C through re-entry and splashdown. Fixed cassette and supernatant reservoir are handed to the customer's courier at 4 C.

Measured in orbit

  • Cell number, morphology and clustering (activated T cells form clusters) Brightfield time-lapse with the bay's LumaScope-class microscope, every 6 h from thaw to the last fixation
  • Post-thaw viability Two-colour live/dead fluorescent stain pumped into the dedicated imaging wells and read on the 2 fluorescence channels
  • Closer look at selected wells Robotic arm moves the bay to the central 40x fluorescence microscope, outside the activation and fixation windows
  • Exact activation and fixation times Time-tagged fluidics valve and pump log
  • Thermal, radiation and acceleration history Bay temperature, pressure and humidity sensors, active silicon dosimeter plus passive TLDs, and the accelerometer log (to flag any fixation that happened during arm motion)

Measured after the return

  • Immediate early gene expression (for example EGR1, EGR2, TNF) and later activation genes (IL2, IFNG, IL2RA) RNA extraction from RNAlater-fixed wells, then RNA-seq or microarray, with qPCR confirmation
  • Activation surface markers (CD69, CD25) and proliferation (Ki-67 or tracking-dye dilution) Flow cytometry on paraformaldehyde-fixed cells
  • Secreted cytokines (IL-2, IFN-gamma, TNF) Multiplex immunoassay on the filtered 24 h supernatants (4 C stability needs ground validation first)
  • Pathway activity (for example Rel/NF-kB, CREB and SRF targets) Analysis of transcription-factor binding sites and pathways in the gene-expression data, compared with the ground control and, if flown, the 1 g centrifuge control

Ground control

Synchronous ground control: an identical flight-model bay and cassette loaded with the same donor cell lots and reagent batches. It replays the same timeline (offset by a few hours) and the recorded flight temperature profile, including the -80 C hold, thaw, 37 C phases and the 4 C return hold. If possible, it also gets a launch vibration profile on a shaker table. Add a pre-launch baseline fixed on the ground. The key flight control in the 2006 and 2014 ISS studies was an on-board 1 g centrifuge running at the same time in KUBIK, which separates microgravity from radiation, launch and handling effects. LELP-1 should fly one (see needs). Without it, the result is flight versus ground only. Because the 2006 flight launched live cells unfrozen, the cryopreserved-launch step should also be checked against fresh cells on the ground.

Coming home

All cells are chemically fixed in orbit, so nothing needs to come back alive. The manufacturer states RNAlater stabilises RNA for 1 month at 4 C (and indefinitely at -20 C), which easily covers the return. The 144 h limit is our estimate and is set by the unfrozen 24 h supernatants: drawn on day 3, they spend about 4 days in orbit, then about 20 min to courier pickup and 1-2 days in transit, so about 6 days at 4 C. The last paraformaldehyde-fixed cells (day 5) reach the lab after about 3-4 days. The bay cannot go below 4 C, so supernatants cannot be frozen in orbit; their cytokine stability for 6 days at 4 C must be checked on the ground before flight. Keep everything at 4 C in transit and avoid temperatures above 25 C.

Limits of this flight

  • Without the added 1 g centrifuge, LELP-1 cannot tell microgravity effects apart from launch, radiation and handling effects. The 2006 and 2014 ISS studies used this control.
  • LELP-1 has no crew and no way to pellet cells. Thawed cells are diluted rather than washed, so some DMSO stays in the medium. Medium changes must go through filters, and activator or fixative mixing relies on pumped flow because there is no sedimentation or convection.
  • Bead-based activators need bead-cell contact, which in microgravity depends on mixing. The protocol therefore uses soluble activators (Con A + anti-CD28, soluble anti-CD3/CD28). Results with bead activation are not directly comparable.
  • Cell attachment changes the outcome: on SLS-1, bead-attached lymphocytes and monocytes responded more in microgravity, not less. Results from suspension wells apply to suspension culture only.
  • There is no CO2 supply. HEPES-buffered medium in sealed wells can drift in pH over several days of activated, high-metabolism culture, so 72 h is about the practical limit without a medium exchange.
  • No in-flight flow cytometry, cytokine assay or RNA readout. In flight, the satellite only gives images and environment logs; the molecular answers come after return.
  • Supernatants cannot be frozen (the bay minimum is 4 C), so cytokine values depend on about 6 days of stability at 4 C.
  • A 2-14 day free-flyer studies isolated cells in vitro. It cannot reproduce astronaut immune changes in the body over months, latent virus reactivation, stress hormones or the role of other tissues.
  • Few donors per flight (2 planned), so donor-to-donor variability limits the statistics. Several flights or more donors in a second bay may be needed.
  • The radiation environment at 550 km sun-synchronous orbit differs from the ISS, so dose comparisons with ISS heritage data need the on-board dosimeter record.

A satellite of its own: SP-IMM B · samples return, hardware burns up

SP-IMM is a 133 kg free-flyer. Only the samples come home: 3.6 kg ride in a 45 kg capsule in a drawer behind an optical window; the microscope looks through the window from the service module, and fluid lines cross through dry-break couplings that part at separation once the last fluid step is done. The microscope, pumps and reservoirs, the centrifuge drive stay in the service module and burn up with it, and a small mechanism moves the fixed cassette off the rotor into the capsule first. Why B: all cells are fixed in orbit; the unfrozen day-3 supernatants set the return clock.

Tailored for this experiment

  • In-flight 1 g centrifuge control (not part of the standard bay): a second identical cassette on a small rotor. At 5 cm radius, 1 g needs about 134 rpm. Without it, the core control from the ISS heritage is missing.
  • Filtered well outlets and a sealed supernatant reservoir, so medium can be harvested in microgravity without drawing up the cells
  • Arm-motion lockout (keeping the vehicle in the 1e-6 g regime) for about 2 h after activator addition and during each fixation
  • A -80 C passive cassette hold time that covers the launch campaign plus 1 day in orbit, with the cassette temperature logged
  • Fixation events time-stamped against the accelerometer and dosimeter logs

Bay or satellite?

The capsule is the floor: no capsule much smaller than about 45 kg has been recovered yet, so 3.6 kg of samples need 133 kg of satellite. On LELP-1 the same samples share one capsule, the whole lab, with 31 other bays. A dedicated satellite earns its place when the experiment needs the schedule to itself, a quieter ride (about 7.3 × 10⁻⁸ g quasi-steady, no robotic arm), or power a bay cannot give.

First-order sizing with a 20 % margin by one model for every experiment, reviewed independently; caveats and sources. The smallest recovered capsule so far (Varda W-1) is under 90 kg, so treat these masses as optimistic.
