Cardiology / drug safety

Human heart muscle cells from stem cells

Some drugs were withdrawn from the market because they caused arrhythmias and sudden death. Drug developers now check cardiac safety on heart muscle cells made from human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSC-CMs), as in the FDA-backed CiPA approach. Spaceflight is linked to a decline in cardiovascular function. On the ISS, flown hiPSC-CMs showed altered calcium handling and irregular beating. Engineered heart tissues also showed weaker contraction and mitochondrial damage, and the CRS-20 study said these changes mimic cardiac aging. Microgravity can stress human heart cells without drugs or genetic changes. This lets researchers study early disease-like changes and test whether compounds protect against them or behave differently.

Who it is for

  • Pharma and CRO cardiac safety teams that already run hiPSC-CM assays (the FDA-backed CiPA approach checks its predictions in these cells)
  • Makers of hiPSC-derived cardiomyocytes and heart-on-a-chip systems who want to show how their models perform in space
  • Academic cardiology, cardiac aging and stem-cell labs
  • Space agencies and commercial crew programmes studying the cardiovascular risks of spaceflight

How LELP-1 runs it

Sample. Commercial or customer hiPSC-derived cardiomyocytes, plated as beating 2D monolayers in a 24-well glass-bottom plate. Optionally, a line that carries a genetically encoded calcium reporter (customer's choice). A 3D engineered-heart-tissue chip is possible only if the customer supplies flight-qualified post-and-sensor hardware. Container. Sealed 24-well glass-bottom cassette (large bay) with gas-permeable lids, connected to the bay fluidics. Reservoirs hold HEPES-buffered maintenance medium, test compound and vehicle, 4% paraformaldehyde and RNA stabilizer, and there is a waste bag. The bay microscope views the cassette from below. Launch. Live, attached and beating at 37 C (late load). Thawing frozen cells in orbit is not recommended. One major supplier (Takara Cellartis) specifies storage at -150 C, the thaw includes a centrifuge step, and cells are ready for use only from day 3 after thawing. Light and gas. Dark except during imaging, when the microscope LED is on for seconds per well. There is no CO2 supply, so the cells use HEPES-buffered medium and gas-permeable lids, with the sealed bay's air headspace supplying oxygen. Medium changes every 2 days keep oxygen and nutrients up and remove lactate.

  1. day -10

    Ground: thaw cells and plate them into the flight cassette. Plate the ground-control cassette from the same lot at the same time. Change medium every 2-3 days.

  2. day -4

    Ground: switch both cassettes to HEPES-buffered medium. Check that every well beats and record baseline video. Seal the cassettes and close the triple containment.

  3. day -1

    Late load into a large bay that is already powered at 37 C. Hold at 37 C through launch.

  4. day 0

    Launch. The bay logs temperature, acceleration and dose.

  5. day 1

    In orbit: first medium exchange. Record baseline brightfield video of all 24 wells (about 10-20 s each), with no arm motion during recording.

  6. day 2

    Daily video of all wells, plus calcium-reporter fluorescence video if a reporter line flies. Repeat every day to day 10.

  7. day 3

    Medium exchange (repeat on days 5, 7 and 9).

  8. day 5

    Mid-mission preservation: 2 wells into 4% PFA and 2 wells into RNA stabilizer. The bay cools these wells only if they sit in a separate cassette zone. Otherwise they stay at 37 C (see limitations).

  9. day 8

    Drug challenge: add the customer's test compound at 2 concentrations, 4 wells each, and vehicle to 4 wells. Record video before dosing and at about 30 min and 2 h after.

  10. day 9

    Medium exchange and final full video set. Optionally, the arm carries the bay to the central 40x microscope for close-up images of reporter cells.

  11. day 10

    End of lab operations: preserve 6 wells in PFA and 6 wells in RNA stabilizer. Keep the remaining 8 wells live at 37 C.

  12. day 11

    Re-entry and splashdown. The bay holds 37 C until handover to the courier on the recovery ship.

Measured in orbit

  • Beat rate and beat-interval irregularity Brightfield video from the bay microscope, analysed with motion-vector analysis as in the CRS-9 study. The spread of beat intervals is the arrhythmia measure.
  • Contraction and relaxation velocity (relative, not absolute force) The same brightfield video, using motion-vector analysis.
  • Calcium transients (only if a calcium-reporter line flies) Fluorescence video on one of the bay microscope's 2 fluorescence channels. The usable kinetics depend on the camera frame rate.
  • Acute response to the test compound Video before and after the fluidics adds the drug or vehicle, compared within the same wells.
  • Cell coverage and morphology Brightfield images, with optional 40x images in the central lab microscope.
  • Environment log Bay temperature, pressure, humidity and acceleration sensors, plus the active silicon dosimeter and passive TLDs.

Measured after the return

  • Whole-transcriptome RNA sequencing RNA-stabilized wells from day 5 and day 10, compared with matched ground wells. Expected readouts are mitochondrial, calcium-handling and stress pathways.
  • Sarcomere structure and cardiac markers Immunofluorescence on PFA-fixed wells (cardiac troponin T, alpha-actinin). Image analysis measures sarcomere length and organisation.
  • Calcium handling and beating in live-return cells Calcium imaging and video contractility on the live wells, starting about 1-3 days after splashdown and repeated up to 10 days to follow recovery.
  • Recovery transcriptome RNA from live-return wells about 10 days after return, as in the CRS-9 design.
  • Spent-medium analysis Medium returned in the waste bag, tested with the customer's choice of assays (for example metabolites or released cardiac proteins).

Ground control

Plate a second cassette from the same cell lot on the same day, with the same HEPES medium, reservoirs, gas-permeable lids and fluidics schedule. Run it in an identical bay on the ground, replaying the flight temperature log and the timing of every medium change, image, drug addition and preservation. If possible, also replay the launch and re-entry vibration and acceleration profiles. Give the live ground wells the same courier hold and the same post-flight analysis. Both heritage studies used matched ground controls. This design has no in-flight 1 g centrifuge control.

Coming home

Live wells must stay at 37 C from the bay to the customer's lab. In CRS-20, live tissues were retrieved two days after splashdown and then hand-carried at 37 C and 5% CO2 to the lab, and they were still usable. LELP-1's plan (handover about 20 min after splashdown, then 1-2 days to the lab) is about 25-49 h, which is close to that heritage. It needs a portable 37 C incubator in the courier chain, and we set a target of 48 h or less. PFA-fixed wells should travel at 4 C. RNA-stabilized wells should travel at 4 C and be frozen at -80 C on arrival.

Limits of this flight

  • Short exposure. The heritage flights lasted about 30 days (CRS-20) and 5.5 weeks (CRS-9), but a LELP-1 mission lasts 2-14 days and sees only early changes. Force loss and mitochondrial damage may not have time to develop. On CRS-9, the beat rate was similar to ground even after 2.5 weeks.
  • No absolute force. Video of 2D monolayers gives beat rate, timing and relative motion, not twitch force. Measuring force needs post-based engineered heart tissues with sensors, which the customer would have to supply and qualify.
  • No CO2. The heritage cultures ran at 5% CO2. LELP-1 uses HEPES-buffered medium, so ground controls must use the same medium, and results are not directly comparable with ISS data.
  • Cells cannot practically launch frozen. One major supplier specifies storage at -150 C (colder than the -80 C passive cassette), the thaw includes centrifugation, and cells are ready only from day 3. A live late load is therefore needed, which adds work in a lab at the launch site.
  • Culture length. The same supplier states cells can be kept for at least 14 days after thawing. This plan needs about 21 days from thaw to splashdown, plus post-flight work. The heritage cultures ran for more than 5 weeks, but the customer must confirm that their line stays stable for that long.
  • Beating and calcium kinetics depend strongly on temperature, so any thermal drift in the bay will confound the results.
  • All live-return samples carry launch and re-entry loads and 1-2 days at 1 g before reaching the lab. Cells readapt early (on CRS-9, gene expression reverted toward ground-like levels after return), so live-return results are partly recovery data.
  • The orbit is 550 km sun-synchronous, so the radiation dose differs from the ISS. Dosimeter data are logged, but radiation and microgravity effects cannot be separated without a 1 g centrifuge control in flight.
  • Few replicates per arm (4-8 wells) and possibly only one cell line per bay. Studying donor-to-donor variability, as in the 3-donor CRS-9 design, needs several bays.
  • No crew. Nobody can fix a well that stops beating, a bubble or a failed medium exchange.

A satellite of its own: SP-HRT C · live return

SP-HRT is a 118 kg free-flyer. The live culture system comes home, powered: 3.1 kg ride in a 45 kg capsule that keeps them at 37 °C on its own battery. The microscope stay in the service module and burn up with it. Why C: live tissues must come back beating at 37 C (CRS-20 heritage); fixed wells need a separate 4 C zone; the microscope stays.

Tailored for this experiment

  • Late load (live cells handed over about 24 h before launch) into a bay that is already powered and held at 37 C through launch, ascent and re-entry, up to handover on the recovery ship
  • An imaging schedule locked out against robotic-arm motion, because vibration corrupts beat analysis from video
  • A video frame rate high enough for beat timing and, if used, calcium transients (the customer sets the minimum; LumaScope-class default modes need checking)
  • Two thermal zones, or a second cassette, if fixed and RNA-stabilized wells are to be cooled to 4 C while live wells stay at 37 C
  • A portable 37 C incubator in the courier chain from ship to lab

Bay or satellite?

The capsule is the floor: no capsule much smaller than about 45 kg has been recovered yet, so 3.1 kg of samples need 118 kg of satellite. On LELP-1 the same samples share one capsule, the whole lab, with 31 other bays. A dedicated satellite earns its place when the experiment needs the schedule to itself, a quieter ride (about 7.3 × 10⁻⁸ g quasi-steady, no robotic arm), or power a bay cannot give.

First-order sizing with a 20 % margin by one model for every experiment, reviewed independently; caveats and sources. The smallest recovered capsule so far (Varda W-1) is under 90 kg, so treat these masses as optimistic.
