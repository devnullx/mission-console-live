Plant biology / space agriculture

Seed germination and root growth

Every crop-growing system for long missions starts with a seed that must sprout, find its way and form a working root. On Earth, gravity tells roots which way to grow, so it is hard to see what other cues (light, touch, internal programs) do on their own. In microgravity that cue is removed, so researchers can see how roots orient, skew and wave without gravity and which genes change. The same early-seedling data also helps breeders and agencies screen seed lines before they commit them to longer crop experiments.

How LELP-1 runs it

Sample. Dry seeds of Arabidopsis thaliana (for example Col-0 and WS, or a GFP reporter line), about 20 seeds per plate. A large-seed crop such as cowpea or moong can replace one plate. Container. Four sealed square seed plates (about 100 mm, nutrient gel or germination membrane) with gas-permeable covers. They are held upright in a fixed orientation in the large-bay cassette frame. Each plate is plumbed to the bay fluidics for hydration and fixation. Launch. Dry, dormant seeds (not hydrated), dark, bay at 18-22 C. Light and gas. White LEDs 16 h on / 8 h off (the photoperiod CROPS used). From day 4, one-sided blue LED light on half the plates for the phototropism test. Dim green or IR light only for imaging in the dark period. No CO2 supply: the bay is sealed with launch air (CROPS started at 20.9% O2 and 400-600 ppm CO2), and O2/CO2 are logged. Seedling biomass at this stage is small.

  1. day -2

    Ground: load dry seeds on plates, seal bay, integrate. Split the same seed lot into a ground control and an unflown dry-seed archive.

  2. day 0

    After orbit checkout: image dry plates, bring bay to 22 C, and hydrate each plate with dilute nutrient solution from the fluidics reservoir. Start white LED 16 h / 8 h and time-lapse imaging every 6 h.

  3. day 2

    Score germination (radicle emergence) from images. Add water only if sensors show plates drying.

  4. day 4

    Switch two plates to one-sided blue light (phototropism test). Two plates stay on white light. Keep the robotic arm idle during this window if possible.

  5. day 6

    Fluorescence imaging of reporter-line plates (if flown) and final morphology image set.

  6. day 7

    Fix in flight: flood two plates with RNA-stabilising fixative (RNAlater-type, for gene expression) and two with an aldehyde fixative (for microscopy). Image after fixation, then set bay to 4 C.

  7. day 8

    Hold at 4 C with lights off until re-entry (any day up to day 14). Hand to the customer's courier on the recovery ship.

Measured in orbit

  • Germination rate and timing Time-lapse macro images every 6 h; count radicle emergence per plate
  • Root length, growth rate, root angle, skewing and waving Image analysis of the time-lapse series (root tracing), as in the ABRS study
  • Phototropic bending of roots and hypocotyls Images before and after one-sided blue light, compared with white-light plates
  • Reporter gene expression (optional) Fluorescence channel of the bay microscope or the central 40x microscope, for GFP reporter lines
  • Growth environment Bay temperature, humidity, pressure, O2/CO2 logging, acceleration sensor and dosimeter (active silicon + passive TLD)

Measured after the return

  • Gene expression RNA extraction from RNA-stabilised seedlings, RNA-seq against ground controls
  • Root cell structure Confocal microscopy of fixed roots stained for cell walls (for example calcofluor white, as in Paul et al. 2012)
  • Final morphology High-resolution scans of fixed plates: root length, angle, root hair and lateral root counts
  • Seed viability and radiation effects Germination test of unhydrated flown seeds against archive seeds; passive TLD dose readout

Ground control

An identical flight-spare module on the ground, loaded from the same seed lot on the same day. It runs the same hydration, light, imaging and fixation timeline at 1 g with plates held vertical, and replays the logged flight temperature, humidity and light record about 1-2 days behind the flight. An unflown dry-seed archive kept at matched temperature separates storage and launch effects from germination effects. A clinostat or random positioning machine run can be added as a simulated-microgravity reference, but it does not replace flight.

Coming home

Samples are fixed in orbit, so they do not need to be alive at return. Keep fixed plates at 4 C from fixation (day 7) to the lab. 336 h covers the time from fixation to re-entry on day 14, plus courier time. This limit is our estimate; check it against the fixative maker's storage data. Live seedlings returned unfixed would no longer reflect the microgravity state after landing.

Limits of this flight

  • 2-14 days covers germination and early seedlings only (up to about the two-leaf stage in crops like cowpea). No flowering, seed-to-seed cycles or crop yield studies.
  • No in-flight 1 g centrifuge, unlike the EMCS on the ISS. Flight and ground differences therefore mix microgravity with launch, radiation and hardware effects.
  • No -80 C freezer for return. Samples are chemically fixed at 4 C, which suits RNA and microscopy but limits some assays (for example, metabolites or enzyme activity may need frozen tissue).
  • Fixing in flight is not proven for this layout. In microgravity, fixative may not cover gel-grown seedlings evenly. Fixative delivery must be tested on the ground and in a flight-like setup first.
  • No crew: plates cannot be swapped, re-wet by hand, or rescued if seeds fail to hydrate. Water behaves differently in microgravity and can bead or bubble.
  • Sealed bay air with no CO2 supply limits plant number and growth stage. Larger seedlings or many seeds would change O2/CO2 levels.
  • In flight, imaging covers whole plates only. Detailed cell-level root data comes only after return.
  • A 550 km sun-synchronous orbit has a different radiation environment from the ISS. Log the dose and compare it before pooling results with ISS data.
  • Any live material after landing has sensed gravity again during the 1-2 day trip. Only samples fixed in flight reflect the microgravity state.

A satellite of its own: SP-PLT B · samples return, hardware burns up

SP-PLT is a 117 kg free-flyer. Only the samples come home: 1.9 kg ride in a 45 kg capsule in a drawer behind an optical window; the microscope looks through the window from the service module, and fluid lines cross through dry-break couplings that part at separation once the last fluid step is done. The microscope, pumps and reservoirs stay in the service module and burn up with it. Why B: seedlings are fixed in orbit; the LED panel and cameras stay; the drawer must keep the plates' orientation to the light.

Bay or satellite?

The capsule is the floor: no capsule much smaller than about 45 kg has been recovered yet, so 1.9 kg of samples need 117 kg of satellite. On LELP-1 the same samples share one capsule, the whole lab, with 31 other bays. A dedicated satellite earns its place when the experiment needs the schedule to itself, a quieter ride (about 7.3 × 10⁻⁸ g quasi-steady, no robotic arm), or power a bay cannot give.

First-order sizing with a 20 % margin by one model for every experiment, reviewed independently; caveats and sources. The smallest recovered capsule so far (Varda W-1) is under 90 kg, so treat these masses as optimistic.
