Bone biology / osteoporosis

Bone cells: resorption and mineralization

On Earth, osteoporosis comes from an imbalance between osteoclasts, which remove bone, and osteoblasts, which build it. In space this imbalance appears quickly: astronauts lose bone mineral density at about 1-1.5% per month in weight-bearing bones. Flight experiments show that isolated bone cells respond to microgravity directly. Osteoclasts resorbed more bone in flight, osteoblasts grew more slowly, and cultured fetal mouse bones mineralized less. A sealed, automated bay lets a lab test this cell-level response, and drugs or materials that might correct it, without crew time.

Who it is for

  • Pharmaceutical and biotech groups developing anti-resorptive or bone-anabolic drugs who want an unloaded cell model
  • Academic bone and mechanobiology labs studying how osteoclasts and osteoblasts sense gravity
  • Space agency human research programmes working on astronaut bone loss countermeasures
  • Bone-graft and resorbable biomaterial companies testing how cells resorb or mineralize their scaffolds
  • Dental and craniofacial research groups using dentin or bone substrates

How LELP-1 runs it

Sample. Two arms in one cassette (our design). Arm A, resorption: an osteoclast precursor line (for example murine RAW 264.7) or human CD14+ monocytes, seeded onto devitalized bovine cortical bone slices or dentin discs, plus calcium-phosphate-coated clear wells for in-flight imaging. Arm B, early mineralization: MC3T3-E1 pre-osteoblasts on clear tissue-culture wells. Container. Large bay 24-well cassette: 8 wells bone/dentin slices (Arm A), 4 wells calcium-phosphate-coated clear wells (Arm A, imaged), 8 wells clear plastic (Arm B), 4 wells cell-free slices and coated wells as background controls. Gas-permeable lids inside the sealed BSL-2 bay. Launch. Cryopreserved (cells in freezing medium in the -80 C passive cassette); slices and coated wells launch dry and sterile; media and reagents in reservoirs. Labile factors (RANKL, M-CSF, ascorbate) need 4 C storage while the bay runs at 37 C (see needs). Light and gas. Dark except during imaging. No CO2: HEPES-buffered alpha-MEM based media, sealed bay air headspace through gas-permeable lids.

  1. day 0

    Launch and orbit insertion. Cells stay frozen in the passive cassette; bay held at 4 C.

  2. day 1

    Automated thaw, dilution and wash; seed Arm A onto slices and coated wells, Arm B onto clear wells. Bay ramps to 37 C. Brightfield image of all clear wells to record seeding density.

  3. day 2

    Medium exchange. Arm A: add RANKL (plus M-CSF if primary monocytes). Arm B: growth medium. Collect first supernatant aliquot from each well.

  4. day 4

    Arm A medium plus RANKL refreshed. Arm B switched to osteogenic medium (ascorbic acid and beta-glycerophosphate) once near confluence. Daily brightfield imaging begins for fusion and spreading.

  5. day 6

    Medium exchange in both arms; supernatant aliquot saved for collagen telopeptide (for example CTX-I) and TRAP5b assays after return. Imaging: count multinucleated cells on coated wells.

  6. day 8

    Medium exchange; add calcein to Arm B medium to label newly deposited mineral. Supernatant aliquot saved. Brightfield imaging of coated wells for cleared areas; calcein fluorescence on Arm B.

  7. day 10

    Medium exchange; supernatant aliquot saved. Full imaging pass of all clear wells; optional robotic-arm transfer to the central 40x fluorescence microscope.

  8. day 11

    Final supernatant collection. Half of each arm fixed with buffered paraformaldehyde (for TRAP, actin, pit and mineral staining); the other half covered with an RNA-stabilizing reagent. Bay ramps to 4 C straight away.

  9. day 12

    Fixative replaced with buffer at 4 C. Bay stays at 4 C through de-orbit, re-entry and splashdown.

Measured in orbit

  • Seeding density and attachment Brightfield imaging of clear and coated wells on day 1 and day 2
  • Osteoclast fusion (multinucleated cells) Daily brightfield imaging of calcium-phosphate-coated clear wells; count large multinucleated cells
  • Resorption areas Brightfield imaging of cleared areas in the calcium-phosphate coating, days 6 to 11, where contrast allows
  • New mineral deposition (Arm B) Calcein fluorescence (green channel) after calcein is added on day 8
  • Higher-resolution cell morphology Optional robotic-arm transfer to the central 40x fluorescence microscope
  • Culture environment Bay temperature, pressure, humidity, seal status and acceleration logs; dose from the active silicon dosimeter and passive TLDs

Measured after the return

  • Bone resorption pit area and depth Remove cells from bone/dentin slices, stain (for example toluidine blue) and image by light microscopy, or by SEM/profilometry
  • Collagen breakdown released into medium Collagen telopeptide assay (for example CTX-I ELISA) on saved supernatants; FOTON-M3 measured collagen telopeptide release as its resorption readout
  • Osteoclast number and maturity TRAP staining of fixed wells; count TRAP-positive multinucleated cells; TRAP5b in supernatants
  • Cytoskeleton and sealing zones Phalloidin (actin) and nuclear staining of fixed wells; confocal imaging
  • Osteoclast and osteoblast gene expression RT-qPCR or RNA-seq of stabilized samples (for example Acp5, Ctsk, Mmp9, Nfatc1, Calcr; Runx2, Sp7, Alpl, Col1a1, Bglap)
  • Osteoblast differentiation and mineral Alkaline phosphatase activity or staining; Alizarin Red S staining and extraction for calcium deposit quantification

Ground control

An identical flight-model module and 24-well cassette, filled from the same cell vials, slice batch and reagent lots, runs on the ground in a spare bay. It replays the flight temperature log and the same fluidics and imaging timeline, offset by a few hours so it can follow the actual flight events. Fixed and stabilized samples are handled and shipped the same way. A synchronous 1 g in-flight centrifuge control, as flown on FOTON-M3 (OCLAST), Bion-10 and IML-2, is the best way to separate microgravity from launch and radiation effects but is not part of the standard bay.

Coming home

All wells are fixed or RNA-stabilized in orbit on day 11, so no live cells need to survive re-entry. Keep the cassette at 4 C from day 11 through recovery and courier transfer (about 20 min after splashdown plus 1-2 days to the lab), which can total about 3 to 6 days. The 168 h limit is our estimate: the RNA-stabilizing reagent maker rates RNA as stable for 1 month at 4 C, but supernatant proteins are less certain. If the total will exceed 168 h, put stabilized samples and supernatants on dry ice or at -80 C on the recovery ship. Fixed slices tolerate longer at 4 C once fixative is replaced with buffer.

Limits of this flight

  • Bone and dentin slices are opaque, so resorption pits on them cannot be seen with the inverted microscope in flight; only the calcium-phosphate-coated clear wells give in-flight resorption data, and their contrast in brightfield may be weak until they are stained after return.
  • Mature mineralized nodules from osteoblast lines usually need longer culture than a 14-day mission allows (our assumption), so Arm B gives early mineralization and differentiation markers, not mature mineralized matrix.
  • The flight evidence for reduced mineralization comes from fetal mouse bone organ cultures, not from osteoblast monolayers; Arm B tests whether a similar effect shows up in a simpler cell model.
  • Cells must be thawed and seeded in orbit; thaw and attachment losses add variability, and the protocol must be proven on the ground first.
  • Without the optional centrifuge, launch loads, radiation at 550 km and handling in the bay cannot be fully separated from microgravity; the ground control covers only temperature and timeline.
  • Isolated cells on slices lack osteocytes, blood supply, hormones and mechanical loading, so results show direct cell responses, not whole-skeleton bone loss.
  • No crew means no slice replacement, no fresh-reagent preparation and no extra fixation time points beyond those planned in the reservoirs.
  • Microgravity is about 1e-4 to 1e-3 g while the robotic arm moves; arm moves should be avoided during the key fusion and resorption days.
  • Samples reach the lab 1-2 days after splashdown, so no live-cell work after return is possible; everything is fixed or stabilized in orbit.

A satellite of its own: SP-BONE B · samples return, hardware burns up

SP-BONE is a 118 kg free-flyer. Only the samples come home: 1.9 kg ride in a 45 kg capsule in a drawer behind an optical window; the microscope looks through the window from the service module, and fluid lines cross through dry-break couplings that part at separation once the last fluid step is done. The microscope, pumps and reservoirs stay in the service module and burn up with it. Why B: wells are fixed or stabilised on day 11 and moved to buffer on day 12, before separation.

Bay or satellite?

The capsule is the floor: no capsule much smaller than about 45 kg has been recovered yet, so 1.9 kg of samples need 118 kg of satellite. On LELP-1 the same samples share one capsule, the whole lab, with 31 other bays. A dedicated satellite earns its place when the experiment needs the schedule to itself, a quieter ride (about 7.3 × 10⁻⁸ g quasi-steady, no robotic arm), or power a bay cannot give.

First-order sizing with a 20 % margin by one model for every experiment, reviewed independently; caveats and sources. The smallest recovered capsule so far (Varda W-1) is under 90 kg, so treat these masses as optimistic.
