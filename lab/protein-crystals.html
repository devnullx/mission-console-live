Protein crystallization for drug formulation

Many drugs are proteins, such as monoclonal antibodies, and today these are usually given by intravenous infusion in hospital. A concentrated, less viscous crystalline suspension could instead be given as an injection under the skin, and better crystals give sharper X-ray structures for drug design. On Earth, sedimentation and convection disturb crystal growth. In microgravity both are reduced, which can give more uniform crystals: on the ISS, Merck got a more uniform, less viscous pembrolizumab suspension, and JAXA got higher-resolution crystals for some proteins.

Who it is for

  • Biologics formulation teams developing high-concentration antibody suspensions for injection under the skin
  • Structural biology and drug-discovery labs that need higher-resolution crystal structures of target proteins
  • Contract research organizations that offer crystallization screening
  • Academic crystallography groups testing how convection affects crystal quality
  • Small-molecule polymorph screening, but only for solution processes that work between 4 and 45 C

How LELP-1 runs it

Sample. A purified, highly homogeneous target protein (for example a monoclonal antibody at 20-40 mg/ml, or a protein for X-ray structure work) with a viscous precipitant such as PEG. The conditions are fixed in ground tests before flight. Container. Large-bay 24-well cassette. 16 sealed batch wells (about 0.5-1 ml each), with protein and precipitant kept in separate reservoirs until the fluidics mix them in orbit. 8 positions hold gel-plugged counter-diffusion capillaries (JAXA style), with the precipitant added by the fluidics at activation. Launch. Live protein solution and precipitant in separate reservoirs, held at 4 C. No crystallization before activation. Not cryopreserved, because freezing would ruin the experiment. Light and gas. No light needed except for brief microscope imaging. No gas exchange: sealed wells, no CO2 or O2 supply.

  1. day -1

    Late load of the filled cassette at 4 C into the powered bay on the pad. A matching ground-control cassette from the same protein lot is loaded at the same time.

  2. day 0

    Launch. Bay holds 4 C. Accelerometer and dosimeter logging start.

  3. day 1

    Arm parked and quiet microgravity confirmed (about 1e-6 g). Baseline brightfield images of all wells. Fluidics mix protein and precipitant in the batch wells and fill the counter-diffusion reservoirs (activation).

  4. day 1

    Temperature step or ramp to the customer's growth setpoint. Default is 20 C, as in JAXA's PCRF. A warming ramp like Merck's (4 C to 30 C) is also possible, with the ramp rate set by the customer.

  5. day 2

    Automated brightfield imaging of chosen wells every 6 h to record nucleation time, crystal count and size. Optional fluorescence if the customer adds a trace-labelled protein.

  6. day 5

    Mid-point check of images on the ground. The customer may change the temperature profile of later wells with a signed command.

  7. day 9

    Last full imaging pass. Bay set to the customer's return hold temperature. No fixative is added, because crystals stay in their mother liquor.

  8. day 10

    End of lab operations. Deorbit, reentry and splashdown with the bay still under thermal control. Cassette handed to the courier about 20 min after splashdown in an active thermal shipper at the same setpoint.

Measured in orbit

  • Nucleation time and crystal count per well Time-lapse brightfield imaging with the bay's LumaScope-class microscope
  • Crystal size and growth rate (crystals larger than about 10 um, our estimate) Image analysis of the time-lapse brightfield frames. Smaller particles are not resolved reliably.
  • Growth front position in counter-diffusion capillaries Brightfield imaging along the capillary
  • Optional protein distribution inside crystals Fluorescence channel, if the customer adds a trace-labelled protein
  • Thermal history Bay temperature sensors, logged against the setpoint
  • Microgravity quality and disturbances Bay accelerometer, time-stamped against arm motion and thruster events
  • Radiation dose Active silicon dosimeter plus passive TLDs
  • Seal integrity Bay pressure and humidity sensors

Measured after the return

  • Particle size distribution of crystalline suspensions Laser diffraction particle sizing, as Merck used
  • Suspension viscosity and sedimentation Viscometry and timed sedimentation, compared with the ground control
  • Crystallinity and protein aggregation SONICC (second-harmonic and UV fluorescence) crystal imaging, and dynamic light scattering of the redissolved protein
  • Bioactivity of dissolved crystals Assay chosen by the customer, for example an ELISA binding assay as Merck used
  • Diffraction resolution and mosaicity X-ray diffraction at a synchrotron beamline, flight vs ground crystals of the same lot
  • Polymorph form (small molecules only) X-ray powder diffraction and thermal analysis

Ground control

A matching cassette from the same protein lot and solutions, loaded at the same time, runs in a flight-equivalent bay on the ground. Telemetry drives its temperature to replay the flight log, including launch, activation, ramp and return-hold phases. It is activated at the same time by the same type of fluidics. Ideally a launch-vibration profile is applied before activation. Ground and flight samples go to the same lab and are analysed blind with the same methods. A third passive set, never activated, can show what launch and return alone do to the solutions.

Coming home

Hold the cassette at the customer's growth or hold temperature (default 20 C, within about +/-2 C) from end of growth to the lab. Do not warm, cool or freeze it unless ground tests show that is safe for that protein. How crystals react to temperature depends on the protein: pembrolizumab crystallized on warming from 4 to 30 C, while JAXA saw space-grown crystals degrade when Soyuz rose above 30 C on return. Handle gently, because crystals sit in liquid and can break under shock. The 72 h limit is our estimate (about 20 min to courier handover plus 1-2 days to the lab) and must be confirmed in ground tests for each protein.

Limits of this flight

  • Lab operations last at most 14 days. Merck's samples spent 18 days in the incubator and JAXA growth took six weeks to four months, so LELP-1 suits only fast-growing systems whose conditions are fixed on the ground beforehand.
  • There is no crew to harvest, mount or flash-cool crystals in orbit. Crystals come back in their mother liquor and can be damaged by reentry and splashdown shocks or by temperature excursions.
  • There is no X-ray diffraction in flight. Crystal quality is known only after return.
  • The bay microscope cannot reliably resolve particles of a few microns, so the in-flight size data are qualitative. The full particle size distribution needs ground instruments.
  • Microgravity helps only some proteins. In JAXA's flights 1 to 5, 40 of 215 samples (19%) gave good results, and success needed highly homogeneous samples in viscous solutions.
  • Sample volumes are millilitres per well. This is a research and formulation-development service, not drug manufacturing.
  • The bay range is 4 to 45 C. Melt-based small-molecule processes such as Varda's W-1 ritonavir run (131 C melt, 80 C growth) are out of scope.
  • Arm motion and any thruster use raise the g-level. Growth wells need quiet periods, and the timeline must be shared with other bays.
  • LELP-1 is a design in a digital twin. None of this has flown, and all LELP-1 numbers are our estimates.

A satellite of its own: SP-XTL B · samples return, hardware burns up

SP-XTL is a 116 kg free-flyer. Only the samples come home: 2.2 kg ride in a 45 kg capsule in a drawer behind an optical window; the microscope looks through the window from the service module, and fluid lines cross through dry-break couplings that part at separation once the last fluid step is done. The microscope, pumps and reservoirs stay in the service module and burn up with it. Why B: crystals return in their mother liquor at the growth temperature, held within about 2 C by heating and cooling (JAXA added 22 C phase-change packs after warm returns).

Tailored for this experiment

  • Powered thermal control on the launch pad and through ascent to hold 4 C after a late load (about 1 day before launch)
  • Continuous, battery-backed thermal control of the bay through deorbit, reentry, splashdown and until courier handover, with no gap in setpoint
  • Active thermal shipper for the courier at the customer's hold temperature, with a temperature logger
  • Quiet-period scheduling: no robotic arm motion during nucleation and growth (arm motion raises the level to about 1e-4 to 1e-3 g)
  • Accelerometer data time-stamped and delivered with the images, so the customer can link disturbances to crystal defects

Bay or satellite?

The capsule is the floor: no capsule much smaller than about 45 kg has been recovered yet, so 2.2 kg of samples need 116 kg of satellite. On LELP-1 the same samples share one capsule, the whole lab, with 31 other bays. A dedicated satellite earns its place when the experiment needs the schedule to itself, a quieter ride (about 7.3 × 10⁻⁸ g quasi-steady, no robotic arm), or power a bay cannot give.

First-order sizing with a 20 % margin by one model for every experiment, reviewed independently; caveats and sources. The smallest recovered capsule so far (Varda W-1) is under 90 kg, so treat these masses as optimistic.
