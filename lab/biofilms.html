Microbiology / materials

Biofilm formation on spacecraft and medical materials

Bacteria that settle on a surface build a slime-covered community called a biofilm. On Earth these films clog pipes and filters and cause device-related infections, and they are much harder to kill than free-floating cells. In spacecraft the same films can grow in water lines and life-support hardware; NASA states that biofilm formation can cause equipment malfunction and human illnesses. Microgravity removes sedimentation and buoyancy-driven flow, which changes how cells and oxygen reach a surface. Two flight experiments with the same strain, run with different growth media, reported opposite results, so the question is still open. A free-flyer lets a materials or device maker fly coupons of their own surface, run an identical module on the ground, and get the fixed coupons back for imaging.

How LELP-1 runs it

Sample. Pseudomonas aeruginosa PA14 (BSL-2 model biofilm organism) plus a customer coupon panel: up to 24 coupons of about 1 cm2, for example stainless steel 316, catheter-grade silicone, a candidate coating and a cellulose membrane reference. Container. 24-well sealed cassette in one large bay. Each well has a glass window on the bottom and holds one coupon above it; three fluid lines serve each well: growth medium, 4% paraformaldehyde, and RNA preservative, plus a shared PBS line for post-fixation washing and staining. Launch. Live inoculum held in stasis in PBS at 4 C (not frozen), diluted to about OD595 0.001; medium, fixative and preservative loaded cold in separate sealed reservoirs; coupons cleaned, sterilised by a method each material tolerates, and mounted in the cassette. Light and gas. No light needed; wells kept dark except during microscope imaging. No CO2 and no gas supply: the cassette is sealed and the medium carries nitrate as a terminal electron acceptor, so growth becomes anaerobic, as in both heritage flights. An aerobic variant with gas-permeable well lids is flown as a separate bay; on the shuttle, more oxygen removed the flight-ground difference.

  1. day 0

    After orbit insertion and attitude settling, the bay ramps from 4 C to 37 C over about 2 h. Pumps fill all 24 wells with medium over the coupons, then inject the stasis inoculum. Static growth starts; first brightfield images and a transmission turbidity reading are taken.

  2. day 1

    Daily automated run: brightfield image stack of each well through the glass window, turbidity reading, and a log of bay temperature, pressure, seal state, dosimeter dose and accelerometer data.

  3. day 2

    Repeat the daily imaging and sensor run. No pumping or arm motion is scheduled, to keep the quasi-steady level near 1e-6 g.

  4. day 3

    Repeat the daily run. Optional single transfer of the bay by the robotic arm to the central 40x microscope for a closer look at two wells; the arm motion is logged because it raises acceleration to about 1e-4 to 1e-3 g.

  5. day 4

    Repeat the daily run.

  6. day 5

    Termination. Twelve wells receive 4% paraformaldehyde and twelve receive RNA preservative, added directly as in the ISS heritage, with no rinse first. After about 1 h the fixed wells are washed with PBS, stained with a nucleic-acid dye and a lipid dye, and imaged on both bay fluorescence channels. The bay then cools to 4 C and holds there.

  7. day 6

    Hold at 4 C. Housekeeping data only; seal and pressure checks confirm containment before deorbit.

  8. day 7

    Deorbit, entry, splashdown and recovery. The sealed cassette stays at 4 C until handover to the customer courier on the recovery ship.

Measured in orbit

  • Biofilm growth over time Daily brightfield image stacks on the bay inverted microscope through each glass window. This shows the film on the window (a reference surface) and on transparent coupons; films on opaque coupons are measured after return
  • Structure at termination Two-channel fluorescence imaging after on-orbit staining of the fixed wells (nucleic-acid dye and lipid dye), optionally repeated at 40x in the central lab microscope
  • Free-floating cell density Transmission turbidity reading per well, as a rough proxy for planktonic growth
  • Culture environment Bay temperature, pressure, humidity and seal sensors logged continuously; confirms the 37 C hold and containment
  • Mechanical and radiation environment Three-axis accelerometer (quasi-steady level and arm-motion events) and active silicon dosimeter plus passive TLDs in the bay
  • Termination timing Valve and pump telemetry records when fixative or preservative reached each well; the ground control is matched to this

Measured after the return

  • Biomass, mean thickness and surface coverage Confocal laser scanning microscopy of the fixed coupons stained for nucleic acids and lipids, quantified with COMSTAT-type image analysis, as in the heritage work
  • Surface topography of film and coupon Scanning electron microscopy on fixed coupons; also shows whether the coating or texture survived the run
  • Gene expression RNA extraction and RNA-seq from the preservative-filled wells, compared with the ground control
  • Attachment pattern on textured or coated surfaces High-resolution confocal microscopy of the patterned and coated coupons to see whether cells attached in or between features
  • Material damage Surface inspection after cleaning and, if the customer asks, profilometry of coupons before and after flight
  • Viable count (optional) Plate counts from one unfixed well. The count reflects survival through two days at 4 C, not the state at day 5

Ground control

An identical flight-spare module runs at the Swiss ground segment with the same cassette, coupons, medium lot and inoculum, started about 2 h after the flight module and terminated on the same offset. The recorded flight temperature profile is played back on the ground unit. A third, non-inoculated cassette is put through the launch vibration and entry load profile to show coupons and seals survive. The bay has no on-orbit centrifuge, so the ground 1 g run is the only gravity reference; the two heritage flights disagreeing shows why that matters.

Coming home

Fixed and preserved wells come back in the sealed cassette at 4 C, the lowest the bay can hold. Fixation is on day 5 and splashdown on day 7; the cassette reaches the courier about 20 min after splashdown and the lab 1-2 days later, so about 4-5 days pass from preservation to the lab. Fixed samples tolerate this; on ISS the fixed samples were held at 4 C for weeks before return. The ISS RNA samples were kept at -80 C on orbit and returned at -20 C; LELP-1 bays cannot freeze, so RNA stays in preservative at 4 C and quality may be lower unless a passive -80 C return cassette is flown.

Limits of this flight

  • Two to fourteen days is short for this field. Spacecraft water-system biofilms build up over months, so the flight shows early attachment and growth, not mature fouling or long-term material damage.
  • No crew. Everything runs on a timer, so there is no re-dosing if a well looks wrong and no fixing a stuck valve.
  • One fixation point per well set. A time course needs several bays or relies on the daily in-flight images, which are lower resolution than post-flight confocal.
  • The bay cannot freeze. RNA stays in preservative at 4 C rather than frozen as on ISS, so transcriptomics may be weaker unless a passive cold cassette is flown.
  • No in-flight 1 g control. Without a centrifuge, the only reference is the ground run, and the heritage flights show flight-versus-ground results on this organism can go either way.
  • Static culture only. The bay has no flow loop, so it does not reproduce flowing water or condensate lines where spacecraft biofilms form.
  • In-flight imaging cannot see films on opaque coupons; those are measured only after return.
  • Entry and splashdown loads act on fixed films. Shear or sloshing can detach or smear them; the non-inoculated cassette on the ground load test is the only check.
  • 24 wells per bay gives a few replicates per material: enough for clear effects, not for small ones or dose-response work.
  • Results may be material-specific. On ISS, biofilm form depended on the material, not on gravity.
  • The column-and-canopy structure is not a safe expectation. It was reported on the shuttle flights and not seen on ISS with the same strain and an anaerobic setup but a different medium, so we do not promise it.

A satellite of its own: SP-BFM B · samples return, hardware burns up

SP-BFM is a 117 kg free-flyer. Only the samples come home: 2.4 kg ride in a 45 kg capsule in a drawer behind an optical window; the microscope looks through the window from the service module, and fluid lines cross through dry-break couplings that part at separation once the last fluid step is done. The microscope, pumps and reservoirs stay in the service module and burn up with it. Why B: biofilms are fixed, washed and stained in orbit before separation; entry loads may shear fixed films.

Tailored for this experiment

  • Glass-window wells and coupon carriers; in-flight imaging sees the window and transparent coupons, opaque coupons are read after return
  • Two termination lines per well, so morphology (paraformaldehyde) and transcriptomics (RNA preservative) come from the same run
  • A PBS line and waste volume for washing fixed wells before on-orbit staining
  • Sealed, gas-tight wells for the anaerobic case, plus a gas-permeable lid option for an aerobic bay; no gas bottle needed
  • On-orbit stain injection (nucleic-acid and lipid dyes) for the fluorescence channels

Bay or satellite?

The capsule is the floor: no capsule much smaller than about 45 kg has been recovered yet, so 2.4 kg of samples need 117 kg of satellite. On LELP-1 the same samples share one capsule, the whole lab, with 31 other bays. A dedicated satellite earns its place when the experiment needs the schedule to itself, a quieter ride (about 7.3 × 10⁻⁸ g quasi-steady, no robotic arm), or power a bay cannot give.

First-order sizing with a 20 % margin by one model for every experiment, reviewed independently; caveats and sources. The smallest recovered capsule so far (Varda W-1) is under 90 kg, so treat these masses as optimistic.
