Stem-cell organoids

Organoids grown from human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) are small 3-D models of human brain or heart tissue. Researchers use them to study diseases such as Parkinson's disease and multiple sclerosis in cells from patients. On the ISS, neural organoids cultured for 30 days had higher levels of maturation genes and lower levels of proliferation genes than ground controls. Cardiac progenitor spheres grew larger and proliferated more than on-orbit 1 g controls. Microgravity gives researchers a different growth condition for studying tissue maturation, disease-linked changes and possible risks to the nervous systems of astronauts.

Who it is for

  • Pharmaceutical and biotech teams that use iPSC-derived organoids in drug discovery for neurodegenerative disease (Parkinson's, multiple sclerosis, Alzheimer's)
  • Academic stem-cell and neuroscience labs, and the disease foundations that fund them
  • Regenerative-medicine developers working on iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes and other cell products
  • Space agencies and human-spaceflight programs that study risks to the central nervous system on long missions
  • Organoid and iPSC tool companies that need flight validation of their culture media, cassettes and assays

How LELP-1 runs it

Sample. iPSC-derived 3-D organoids made in the customer's lab. The reference case is cortical or dopaminergic neural organoids, with or without matched microglia, as on the 2019 ISS flight. Cardiac progenitor spheres are a second option. One organoid goes in each well, with matched patient and control lines spread across the cassette. Container. LELP-1 large-bay 24-well sealed culture cassette with about 1 mL of medium per well (the heritage flight used 1 mL per sealed cryovial). It connects to the bay fluidics, with reservoirs for medium, 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) fixative, PBS storage buffer, RNA stabilizer and waste. The bay provides BSL-2 triple containment. Launch. Live organoids in sealed wells with HEPES-buffered medium, loaded late, with the bay held at 37 C and powered through ascent. On the 2019 ISS flight the organoids were shipped at 4 C and flew live in a 37 C incubator. Option for cardiac spheres: launch cryopreserved at -80 C in the passive cassette and thaw automatically in orbit. On the 2020 heritage flight, the thaw was done by hand on board, so an automated thaw has no flight heritage. Light and gas. No light is needed except short LED exposures for imaging. There is no CO2 supply. Wells use HEPES-buffered medium (heritage: DMEM/F-12 with 15 mM HEPES) and a sealed air headspace, with no gas exchange during flight. On the 2019 flight, organoids in sealed 1 mL cryovials showed no signs of hypoxia after 30 days.

  1. day -3

    The customer lab finishes the organoids (aggregation, regional differentiation, and microglia added where used). The batch is split evenly between the flight cassette and the ground-control cassette.

  2. day -2

    Organoids are shipped at 4 C to the integration site. The 2019 heritage team shipped to Kennedy Space Center at 4 C.

  3. day -1

    One organoid is loaded into each well with 1 mL of HEPES-buffered medium. The cassette is sealed and installed in the bay at a 37 C setpoint, and the bay stays powered from here on. Spare organoids from the batch are fixed on the ground as the launch baseline.

  4. day 0

    RUPAK launch with the bay held at 37 C. Logging of acceleration, temperature and radiation dose starts.

  5. day 1

    In-orbit checkout: seal and pressure check, then a brightfield image of every well as the in-orbit baseline.

  6. day 2

    Daily brightfield imaging of all live wells for organoid size and shape, timed outside robotic-arm moves. Fluorescence images are taken if the customer uses reporter lines.

  7. day 3

    Early time point: in 4 wells, the medium is pumped to waste and replaced with RNA stabilizer. The 2020 cardiac flight fixed samples in RNAProtect at day 3.

  8. day 7

    Optional half-medium exchange (0.5 mL) in the live wells from the medium reservoir. Skip it to match the no-exchange heritage protocol. The arm carries the bay to the central 40x microscope for high-resolution images of 2 to 4 wells.

  9. day 12

    Last full imaging round of all live wells.

  10. day 13

    End of culture. In 8 wells the medium is replaced with 4% PFA for histology. In 6 wells it is replaced with RNA stabilizer. 6 wells stay live in their own medium for return, and that medium carries the secreted proteins.

  11. day 13.2

    After about 4 hours of fixation, the PFA in the 8 histology wells is pumped to waste and replaced with PBS storage buffer to avoid over-fixation during the trip home.

  12. day 13.5

    The bay setpoint is ramped from 37 C to 4 C and held. Imaging and pumping stop during the ramp so power stays under the peak limit.

  13. day 14

    Deorbit and splashdown, with the bay held at 4 C on battery. The cassette goes to the customer's courier about 20 minutes after splashdown and reaches the lab at 4 C within 1 to 2 days.

Measured in orbit

  • Organoid size, shape and survival over time Daily LumaScope-class brightfield images of every live well, then image analysis of area and outline
  • Reporter signals (e.g. labelled microglia or neuronal reporters), if the customer uses them The two fluorescence channels of the bay microscope
  • High-resolution structure in a few wells The arm carries the bay to the central 40x fluorescence microscope. The higher g level while the arm moves (about 1e-4 to 1e-3 g) is logged.
  • Radiation dose to the cassette Active silicon dosimeter (time-resolved), plus passive TLDs read after landing. The 2019 study used station radiation logs to separate radiation effects from microgravity effects.
  • Culture environment and microgravity quality Bay temperature, pressure, humidity and accelerometer logs, plus containment seal sensors

Measured after the return

  • Gene expression (maturation, proliferation, stress, inflammation) RNA sequencing of the organoids stabilized in orbit (day 3 and day 13), compared with ground-control and launch-baseline organoids. The heritage study used RNA sequencing on organoids snap-frozen after return.
  • Tissue structure and cell types Cryosectioning and immunostaining of the PFA-fixed organoids (nuclear stain, neuronal, glial and proliferation markers)
  • Secreted proteins Proximity extension assay on medium from the live-return wells. The heritage study used Olink Explore 1536.
  • Viability and neurite outgrowth Live-return organoids are plated on coated dishes and cultured at 37 C. In the heritage study, returned organoids attached and grew neurite networks.
  • Neuronal network activity Multi-electrode array recordings after the returned organoids are plated. The heritage study used MEA only on ground organoids to validate the cryovial method, not on flown organoids.
  • Cardiac function (cardiac-sphere option) Return of beating, beat rate and calcium transient imaging after recovery in an incubator

Ground control

An identical flight-spare large-bay module and 24-well cassette, filled from the same organoid batch on the same day and kept at 37 C in the customer's or the integrator's lab. It runs the same fluidics timeline: RNA stabilization on day 3, an optional exchange on day 7, PFA fixation and RNA stabilization on day 13, the swap from PFA to PBS, and the cool-down to 4 C. It runs a few hours behind the flight so it can replay the flight bay's temperature log, and it goes through the same 4 C hold and courier trip. Launch-baseline organoids fixed on day -1 show the starting state. The standard bay has no in-orbit 1 g centrifuge, so the only microgravity control is on the ground. Launch vibration, radiation and 1 g are not matched; the dosimeter and accelerometer logs are used to account for them.

Coming home

The organoids are fixed or RNA-stabilized in orbit on day 13, before deorbit, so re-entry and the 1 g trip home do not change the end-point. Fixed organoids are moved from PFA to PBS after about 4 hours to avoid over-fixation. Live-return wells are cooled to 4 C, the temperature the heritage team used to ship live organoids before launch. The 72-hour window from preservation to the lab is our planning value: about 1 day in orbit, about 20 minutes to handover, then 1 to 2 days by courier. In the 2019 heritage flight, the CubeLab was opened six days after undocking.

Limits of this flight

  • LELP-1 runs at most 14 days. The neural-organoid heritage flights lasted about 30 days, so changes seen after a month may be smaller or missing at 14 days. The 3-day cardiac data suggest that early gene changes can be measured.
  • The standard bay has no in-orbit 1 g centrifuge. Microgravity effects cannot be separated from launch, radiation and hardware effects as cleanly as with the centrifuge control in the ISS MVP.
  • Mature brain organoids launch live, not frozen, which needs a powered late load and a short pad timeline. We did not find a flight-proven method to cryopreserve whole mature neural organoids and thaw them automatically. The cryopreserved option has flight heritage only for cardiac progenitor spheres, and that thaw was done by hand on the ISS.
  • There is no crew on board. Bubbles, a blocked line or a leaking well cannot be fixed by hand, so the protocol depends on the automated fluidics working the first time.
  • The bay has no CO2 supply. The culture relies on HEPES buffering in sealed wells, which limits how long and how dense the culture can be before pH and oxygen drift. Without the optional sensors, pH and oxygen are not measured in flight.
  • With 24 wells and one organoid per well, groups are small once lines, microglia conditions and time points are split. The heritage authors said their protein data lacked statistical power.
  • Live-return organoids spend up to about 3 days at 4 C after the flight. Cold storage changes gene expression, so only organoids fixed or RNA-stabilized in orbit give a clean flight end-point.
  • Electrophysiology (multi-electrode arrays) and single-cell sequencing happen only after return. The bay cannot record neural activity in flight.
  • Each robotic-arm move raises the g level to about 1e-4 to 1e-3 g, so imaging at the central microscope slightly disturbs the microgravity condition.
  • The BOARDS (Muotri) results quoted here come from ISS National Lab reports. We did not find a peer-reviewed flight paper for them.

A satellite of its own: SP-ORG B · samples return, hardware burns up

SP-ORG is a 117 kg free-flyer. Only the samples come home: 1.7 kg ride in a 45 kg capsule in a drawer behind an optical window; the microscope looks through the window from the service module, and fluid lines cross through dry-break couplings that part at separation once the last fluid step is done. The microscope, pumps and reservoirs stay in the service module and burn up with it. Why B: fixed and stabilised organoids and 6 live wells come back at 4 C; plating, neurite outgrowth and network activity need the live wells.

Tailored for this experiment

  • The bay must stay powered at 37 C from cassette sealing on day -1, through integration, the launch pad and ascent, because the organoids launch live. This late-load, powered-ascent service goes beyond a passive cryo launch.
  • The bay must hold 4 C on battery through deorbit, splashdown and handover, and a 4 C insulated courier shipper must be ready on the recovery ship.
  • Robotic-arm moves and attitude manoeuvres must be scheduled outside imaging windows, with acceleration logged for every well image.
  • Optional, not standard: a small on-orbit 1 g centrifuge cassette as an in-flight gravity control, like the MVP hardware on the 2020 cardiac flight. This would need a dedicated large bay.
  • Optional, not standard: optical pH and oxygen sensor spots in the wells, read by the bay microscope, to monitor the sealed HEPES culture.

Bay or satellite?

The capsule is the floor: no capsule much smaller than about 45 kg has been recovered yet, so 1.7 kg of samples need 117 kg of satellite. On LELP-1 the same samples share one capsule, the whole lab, with 31 other bays. A dedicated satellite earns its place when the experiment needs the schedule to itself, a quieter ride (about 7.3 × 10⁻⁸ g quasi-steady, no robotic arm), or power a bay cannot give.

First-order sizing with a 20 % margin by one model for every experiment, reviewed independently; caveats and sources. The smallest recovered capsule so far (Varda W-1) is under 90 kg, so treat these masses as optimistic.
