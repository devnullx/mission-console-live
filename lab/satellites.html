A SATELLITE PER EXPERIMENT

Fifteen dedicated free-flyers, one per experiment

Every experiment in the catalog can fly as a bay on LELP-1 or on a satellite of its own. Each satellite here is sized by the same first-order model: a service module with array, battery, radios and a deorbit system, and a return capsule that carries the experiment home under a heat shield. 12 of them land between 78 and 85 kg: the payloads are similar, so one common bus with an experiment-specific capsule would serve them. The 3 heavier ones carry what a bay cannot: SP-ALY (139 kg), SP-MECH (113 kg), SP-IMM (108 kg).

What the numbers say

  • A single-module dedicated satellite launches about 13 to 15 times its payload's mass. On LELP-1 the same payload takes one of 32 bays in a 242.6 kg lab, about 7.6 kg of lab per bay. For one 6 kg experiment, the shared lab is the efficient choice.
  • Dedicated flights earn their place when an experiment needs what a bay cannot give: the metallic furnace (about 200 W during melt-back), a 1 g centrifuge next to the samples, the schedule to itself, or a quieter ride with no robotic arm on board.
  • The single-module designs differ by less than 7 kg: one common bus with experiment-specific capsules is the practical product line.

How they are sized

Payload power = module peak + a 25 W thermoelectric block per module (average: 60% of peak + 12 W per block; the furnace slot has a radiator instead). Array power = margin × Pavg × (f/Xe + (1 − f)/Xd) / (1 − f), the SMAD form with peak-power-tracking path efficiencies Xd 0.8, Xe 0.6, sized for the worst eclipse fraction f of the 550 km dawn-dusk orbit at the aphelion-season solar flux. Battery = the largest of the eclipse at 40% depth of discharge, 3.5 h of ascent before the arrays deploy, a time-stepped furnace run (array power only in sunlight), and a 100 Wh minimum pack. Capsule mass = (payload + capsule avionics, thermal and an 8 h hold battery) / (1 − heat shield − recovery − structure fractions) × (1 + margin); diameter from the ballistic coefficient, D = √(4m / (π CD β)), matched to LELP-1's return module. Dry mass = (capsule + (bus items + propulsion dry) × (1 + margin)) / (1 − structure − harness fractions); propellant from the rocket equation for 190 m/s with the capsule attached; enough 22 N thrusters to finish the burn within a tenth of an orbit. Quasi-steady g: gravity gradient 2n²r for a Sun-pointing attitude and drag with the arrays at the worst-season angle.

Independent review and caveats

Three independent reviews checked the model: the physics and maths, every parameter against published sources, and the designs as a spacecraft systems engineer would. All three found it sound with fixes, and the fixes are in: array sizing with separate daylight and eclipse path efficiencies (SMAD, peak-power tracking) at the aphelion-season solar flux, a 20 % system margin, heavier capsule fractions, a 3.5 h ascent hold, an 8 h capsule hold, a delta-v reserve, a propulsion module sized like a flown green-monopropellant unit, a thrust-limited deorbit burn, a time-stepped furnace battery case and a counter-rotor for the centrifuge designs. The deorbit delta-v, the eclipse geometry, the capsule-diameter relation and the microgravity formulas were confirmed.

  • These are first-order, parametric designs with a 20 % system margin, not engineering designs. Flown recoverable free-flyers are heavier than these numbers suggest (Varda's W-series spacecraft is about 300 kg with a capsule nearly 1 m wide; ATMOS Phoenix 1 was about 250 kg), so treat the masses as optimistic.
  • At the same ballistic coefficient as LELP-1, a capsule of 0.9 to 1.3 m sees roughly 1.4 to 1.7 times LELP-1's peak heat flux because of its smaller nose. Below about 1 m a rigid ablative capsule, as Varda flies, may be the better choice than an inflatable; the inflatable is shown for commonality with LELP-1.
  • An unguided capsule lands tens of km from its aim point, mostly along track, so finding and lifting it takes hours; the capsule holds its samples at temperature for 8 h. A parachute is mandatory for live samples.
  • Rideshare launchers usually integrate payloads days before launch and keep them unpowered on the pad and during ascent. Live, late-loaded biology needs a late-access arrangement; the 3.5 h ascent battery case covers only the time after liftoff.
  • Microgravity numbers are quasi-steady only (gravity gradient and drag, about 10⁻⁷ g). Vibration from reaction wheels, pumps and especially a spinning 1 g centrifuge rotor will dominate, at roughly 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻³ g. Drag can be several times higher at high solar activity.
  • A 1 g rotor carries about 0.1 N m s of angular momentum, more than small-satellite wheels hold; the centrifuge designs carry a counter-rotor or larger wheels.
  • {'The service module follows the capsule into the entry corridor unless it diverts after separation; it must burn up away from the recovery ship. A failed deorbit burn would leave the satellite at 550 km for years': 'debris rules and biological containment of the capsule apply.'}
  • Every design is sized for the worst eclipse season of the dawn-dusk orbit (about 22 min of shadow per orbit around the June solstice, 3 May to 9 August); the rest of the year the orbit stays in sunlight.
