LELP-1: design of a free-flying life-science lab

LELP-1 is a free-flying, uncrewed laboratory for life science and microgravity research. It flies 32 sealed experiment bays in a 550 km dawn-dusk orbit for as long as the longest experiment on board needs, up to 14 days, runs them automatically on signed customer commands, and brings the whole lab home under an inflatable heat shield so samples reach the customer's courier within half an hour of splashdown.

Mission

LELP-1 is the orbital laboratory of the RUPAK system. The reusable RUPAK booster and its upper stage put the lab into a 550 km dawn-dusk sun-synchronous orbit. The upper stage stays attached during the mission: it carries the solar wings, trims the orbit and later flies the deorbit burn. Lab operations last as long as the longest protocol on the manifest (2 to 14 days). Then the robotic arm parks itself on the stage, the lab separates, inflates its 2.6 m heat shield, enters, opens its parachute at 4 km and splashes down near the recovery ship. The arm, the solar wings and the relay antenna stay with the stage, so 222.6 kg comes home and the stage breaks up over open sea.

Quasi-steady accelerations at a bay are below one millionth of Earth's gravity in the twin's numbers and are set by the gravity gradient, because the bays sit about 2 m from the centre of mass of the lab and upper-stage stack; drag at 550 km is about a hundred times smaller. That is the same order as the ISS's quasi-steady level, so a free-flyer's real gain is in vibration and transients: no crew, no dockings, no station machinery. LELP-1 has its own disturbances: in the twin the robotic arm shakes the lab to about 10⁻⁴ to 10⁻³ g while it moves (estimate), and reaction wheels and pumps add vibration. The mission plan therefore keeps quiet windows (no arm moves during crystallisation, solidification or mechanobiology steps), and every bay logs its own acceleration so customers see what their samples felt. These LELP-1 levels are model estimates until a bay accelerometer flies.

Radiation

The twin's dosimeter model gives about 0.6 mGy per day inside a bay at 550 km, with about six South Atlantic Anomaly passes a day, so a 7-day flight collects a few mGy. Measured doses inside the ISS Columbus module were about 0.2 to 0.36 mGy per day (table below), so the model is 1.7 to 3 times higher: plausible for a higher orbit with less shielding, but a model value until the dosimeter flies. Every bay logs its own dose from lab power-on to entry, and passive TLDs integrate the whole flight. A few mGy is far below the doses of most in-vitro radiation studies, so on LELP-1 microgravity dominates the space-versus-ground difference; a 1 g centrifuge bay on board separates the two.

Lessons from earlier platforms

  • Get return approvals early (Varda W-1): the capsule launched in June 2023 but its FAA reentry license came only on 14 February 2024, and reporting cites license delays. LELP-1 should hold its reentry, range and splashdown permits before launch so the 2-14 day lab clock is real.
  • Drop-test the whole descent chain (The Exploration Company Mission Possible): the capsule reentered under control but was lost after communications cut at 26 km, before parachute opening; the chutes had not been drop-tested. LELP-1 should qualify the inflatable heat shield, parachute and flotation end to end before flying customer samples.
  • Plan the splash zone around the ships (ATMOS Phoenix 1): the inflatable heat shield flew, but the capsule splashed about 2,000 km offshore and recovery was not planned. LELP-1 must target a zone where the recovery ship already waits, to keep the 2 h courier handover.
  • Hand samples over fast (Bion-M No.1): mice were examined about 3 h after landing and showed some motor recovery by 6-8 h, so timing changed the readout. LELP-1 should log time from splashdown to fixation for every sample and offer in-flight fixation before reentry.
  • Monitor every bay so faults can be told apart from biology (Foton-M No.4 and Bion-M No.1): all Foton-M4 geckos died and the cause was still being assessed at landing; on Bion-M No.1, 15 mice died from a food-system failure. LELP-1 should log video, temperature, gas and fluidics state per bay, and consider a 1 g centrifuge control bay.
  • Run an identical ground twin (EcAMSat): flight results were compared with an identical payload run in the lab at NASA Ames. LELP-1 should run a synchronous ground bay with the same cassette, media and timeline for every customer.
  • Use proven autonomous well and card assays (PharmaSat, EcAMSat, BioSentinel): automated culture with dosing and in-flight readout worked without crew. LELP-1's 12- and 24-well cassettes with automated fluidics follow this path; downlink key data in flight so the mission still yields results if return fails.
  • Pair dose with biology (BioSentinel): a TimePix-based spectrometer flew next to the yeast to link dose and response. LELP-1 should keep an active dosimeter and passive TLDs in each bay and report dose per sample.
  • Hold temperature through the whole timeline (POEM-4 CROPS, Bion-M No.1): CROPS kept 20-30 C and Bion-M held about 21 C. LELP-1's 4-45 C bay control should stay powered through reentry, float and ship transfer, with a logged temperature trace handed to the customer.
  • Decide what must come back (SJ-10): only the return capsule with 11 loads came home while 8 loads kept running on the orbital module. LELP-1 returns the whole 243 kg lab, which simplifies sample handling but costs mass; this is a positioning point, not just a feature.
  • Offer data access like a station facility (ICE Cubes): customers log in remotely 24 hours a day and send commands. LELP-1's ground segment should give customers the same live view and signed command path.
  • Position against no-return platforms (POEM-4): stage platforms give orbit time at about 350 km but no sample return. LELP-1's value is return of live or fixed samples within days; say so plainly.

Open design items

  • Power for the new bay types (lit bay, centrifuge, furnace, freezer) has to fit the 600 W array with the platform loads; the twin has not yet budgeted them.
  • Reaction wheels are sized for the robotic arm (3 x 3.5 N m s); a slower arm would allow smaller wheels and less vibration.
  • A manifest with every bay at its limit would weigh about 281 kg, above the 250 kg allocation; every manifest is checked.
  • Bay accelerometers and the quiet-window plan need a vibration analysis of the arm, wheels and pumps.
  • Entry loads on samples (7.7 g peak in the entry model) and splashdown shock need qualification for each bay type.
  • The payload partner for bay hardware (fluidics, imagers) is not decided; the twin assumes Space Philic builds the standard bay.
