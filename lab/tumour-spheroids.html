Oncology

3D tumour spheroids for drug testing

Most early cancer drug tests use flat (2D) cell layers. Gene expression in 3D spheroids is much closer to clinical profiles than in 2D, so spheroids are a better model for drug screening. In microgravity, cancer cells can gather into 3D spheroids on their own, without a scaffold, as human thyroid cancer cells did on Shenzhou-8 in 2011 and on the ISS in 2017-2018. NASA has also run an ISS investigation of antibody-drug conjugates on cancer cells in microgravity. A recoverable free-flyer lets a customer grow these spheroids, dose them with a drug, fix them in orbit, and compare them with an identical ground control.

How LELP-1 runs it

Sample. Human cancer cell line chosen by the customer (for example FTC-133 follicular thyroid cancer cells, as flown before), about 1e5-5e5 cells per well. Optional fluorescent reporter line for in-flight imaging. Container. Large bay, 24-well cassette with gas-permeable lids, linked to the bay fluidics (medium, vehicle, drug, fixative and RNA-stabiliser reservoirs, waste bag). Well surface chosen by the customer: standard surfaces if the question is whether microgravity alone drives spheroid formation, or low-attachment surfaces for more even spheroids (these also form spheroids at 1 g). Launch. Cryopreserved at -80 C in the passive cryo cassette; thawed, washed free of cryoprotectant and seeded automatically in orbit. Light and gas. Dark except during imaging. No CO2: HEPES-buffered medium in the sealed bay with gas-permeable lids. No robotic-arm moves during the aggregation days if possible, to keep about 1e-6 g. A lower setpoint (CellBox-2 used 28 C) can be chosen to slow growth.

  1. day 0

    Launch. Cells held at -80 C in the passive cryo cassette. Bay pre-conditioned at 37 C with medium reservoirs filled.

  2. day 1

    Automatic thaw, dilution and wash to remove cryoprotectant; seed cell suspension into all 24 wells. Baseline brightfield image of every well.

  3. day 2

    Free aggregation in microgravity. Brightfield imaging of each well every 12 h to count aggregates and measure their size.

  4. day 4

    Imaging continues. Bay logs temperature, pressure, acceleration and dose so aggregation can be linked to any disturbance.

  5. day 5

    Automatic partial medium exchange in all wells (Shenzhou-8 and CellBox-2 both exchanged medium 5 days into the flight).

  6. day 6

    Dosing: 8 wells vehicle only, 8 wells low drug dose, 8 wells high drug dose, pumped from sealed drug reservoirs. Image all wells before and 2 h after dosing.

  7. day 7

    Daily brightfield and fluorescence imaging (reporter or viability dye) to follow spheroid size and outline under drug. Optional: arm moves the bay to the central 40x microscope for close-up images.

  8. day 10

    Add a viability dye to a subset of wells and image. Then fix: 12 wells with RNA stabiliser (for RNA and protein), 12 wells with buffered paraformaldehyde (for imaging and histology), 4 of each per dose group.

  9. day 11

    Replace paraformaldehyde with buffer after about 24 h to avoid over-fixation. Set the bay to 4 C and hold until handover to the courier.

Measured in orbit

  • Spheroid count, size and shape over time Brightfield imaging with the bay's LumaScope-class microscope, every 12-24 h, automated image analysis of area and outline
  • Drug response trend (growth delay, loss of compact outline) Change in spheroid area and outline in drug wells against vehicle wells, from the same daily images
  • Viability or reporter signal Two-channel fluorescence imaging of a reporter cell line or a dye added through the fluidics before fixation
  • Close-up structure of selected spheroids Robotic arm carries the bay to the central 40x fluorescence microscope
  • Environment record Bay temperature, pressure, humidity and acceleration sensors; active silicon dosimeter and passive TLDs

Measured after the return

  • Spheroid size, number and morphology Stereo and light microscopy of fixed wells; comparison with the ground control
  • Internal structure and drug effect Histology and immunofluorescence of sectioned spheroids (for example proliferation, cell-death and hypoxia markers)
  • Gene expression qPCR or RNA sequencing from RNA-stabilised wells, including genes reported before (EGF, CTGF, VCL, PXN, ITGB1, RELA, ERK1/2)
  • Protein changes Western blot or proteomics from RNA-stabilised wells
  • Dose received Read-out of passive TLDs from the bay

Ground control

An identical large bay and cassette, with the same cell batch, medium, drug doses and fixatives, runs on the ground at 1 g. It replays the flight timeline and temperature log from telemetry, a few hours behind the flight. This follows CellBox-2, where ground controls used the same temperature and time profiles as the flight. A second ground set on a random positioning machine (simulated microgravity) is optional. Without an in-flight centrifuge, launch, re-entry and radiation effects cannot be fully separated from microgravity.

Coming home

Samples are fixed in orbit, so no live cells need to come back. On CellBox-2, fixed cells were stored at 4 C. Hold the bay at 4 C through re-entry, recovery and courier transport. Paraformaldehyde is replaced with buffer in orbit to prevent over-fixation. 96 h is our estimate; the customer should confirm it for their assays.

Limits of this flight

  • Spheroid formation is not guaranteed. The CellBox-2 paper reports that no spheroids formed in the earlier CellBox-1 mission, where cells had become a confluent monolayer after launch delays.
  • Lab time is 14 days at most. A standard ground spheroid screen (spheroid start, drug treatment, integrity and survival endpoints) takes about 170 h and fits. Measuring growth delay and regrowth after a drug needs 14 days or more of culture, which LELP-1 cannot offer after the thaw and aggregation days.
  • Spheroids that form freely in microgravity vary in size and number. This makes drug-response data less uniform than plate-based ground screens.
  • Large spheroids (millimetres across) develop gradients of oxygen and nutrients. This affects drug penetration, and the bay microscope cannot see inside them.
  • No crew: wells cannot be checked by hand, individual spheroids cannot be picked, and doses cannot be changed except by pre-loaded reservoirs.
  • Cells launch frozen and are thawed in orbit. Thaw stress and launch loads may affect early behaviour.
  • No CO2 supply: HEPES-buffered medium only, and pH may drift over 10 days.
  • Without an in-flight 1 g control, radiation, launch and re-entry effects cannot be fully separated from microgravity.
  • Cells are fixed in orbit, so live spheroids cannot be brought back for regrowth tests or further drug treatment.
  • The two heritage flights cited here used one thyroid cancer cell line (FTC-133). Results may not carry over to other tumour types.
  • Neither heritage flight tested a drug on the spheroids. Drug dosing here is the LELP-1 proposal, not a flown result.

A satellite of its own: SP-ONC B · samples return, hardware burns up

SP-ONC is a 118 kg free-flyer. Only the samples come home: 2.0 kg ride in a 45 kg capsule in a drawer behind an optical window; the microscope looks through the window from the service module, and fluid lines cross through dry-break couplings that part at separation once the last fluid step is done. The microscope, pumps and reservoirs stay in the service module and burn up with it. Why B: spheroids are fixed in orbit and moved to buffer the next day, before separation.

Tailored for this experiment

  • A low-magnification brightfield objective or a wide-field mode, because spheroids can reach several millimetres (5-10 mm were reported after Shenzhou-8) and outgrow a normal field of view
  • Image-analysis software on board or on the ground for spheroid area and count
  • Cytotoxic-drug handling: drug reservoirs and waste kept inside the triple containment, with a materials check that fixatives and drugs do not react with the wetted parts (Shenzhou-8 hardware materials were tested not to inactivate RNAlater)
  • No robotic-arm moves near the bay during the aggregation days (days 1-5), to keep the quasi-steady level near 1e-6 g
  • Power after splashdown so the bay can hold 4 C until the courier handover

Bay or satellite?

The capsule is the floor: no capsule much smaller than about 45 kg has been recovered yet, so 2.0 kg of samples need 118 kg of satellite. On LELP-1 the same samples share one capsule, the whole lab, with 31 other bays. A dedicated satellite earns its place when the experiment needs the schedule to itself, a quieter ride (about 7.3 × 10⁻⁸ g quasi-steady, no robotic arm), or power a bay cannot give.

First-order sizing with a 20 % margin by one model for every experiment, reviewed independently; caveats and sources. The smallest recovered capsule so far (Varda W-1) is under 90 kg, so treat these masses as optimistic.
