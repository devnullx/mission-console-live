Microbiology / infectious disease

Antibiotic resistance of pathogenic bacteria

Drug-resistant bacteria are a large cause of death on Earth: WHO estimates bacterial antimicrobial resistance was associated with more than 4.7 million deaths globally in 2021. In spaceflight the problem has a second edge, because dysregulation of the human immune system persists for the duration of a 6-month orbital flight, so an infection that is routine on the ground is harder to treat. Flight data show the bacteria change too, and they do not all change the same way: Salmonella flown on STS-115 had 167 genes differentially expressed and killed mice faster, E. coli grown on the ISS reached a 13-fold higher final cell count than its ground control with smaller cells and thicker envelopes, yet on the EcAMSat free-flyer uropathogenic E. coli came out more susceptible to gentamicin in microgravity, not less. Microgravity is worth using here because it removes sedimentation and buoyancy-driven convection, so each cell sits in a quiet, diffusion-limited layer of its own waste and of the drug, a condition that is hard to hold steady for days on the ground; the disagreement between the flight results so far is the reason to run more of these experiments, not fewer.

  • Academic microbiology and infectious disease labs with modeled-microgravity results that need a real flight test

How LELP-1 runs it

Sample. Uropathogenic E. coli, BSL-2 clinical isolate, flown as a matched pair: wild type and an isogenic deletion mutant of the stress-response sigma factor gene rpoS. 4 gentamicin levels (0, 25, 55, 100 micrograms/mL) x 2 strains x 3 wells = 24 wells. Container. One 24-well sealed microfluidic cassette in a large bay, about 400 microlitres per well, each well with its own inlet, outlet and LED/photodiode pair. BSL-2 triple containment with an absorbent secondary tray and a seal pressure sensor. Launch. Live cells, no cryopreservation: loaded at the launch site within 24 h of liftoff and held dormant at 4 C in the sealed wells, with growth medium kept in a separate reservoir and not in contact with the cells. No thaw step and no -80 C cassette. Load-to-dose time is kept to days, because EcAMSat reported that multi-week storage in payload hardware stressed the cells. Light and gas. No light needed for growth; the only light is the 570 nm and 600 nm LED pairs used for the dye readout, plus the microscope illuminator. No CO2 supply needed: the medium is HEPES-buffered and the sealed air headspace is sized for the well volume. Headspace oxygen is monitored, not replenished.

  1. day 0

    Launch. Bay held at 4 C, cells dormant, seal and pressure logged every minute. Dosimeter running.

  2. day 1

    Orbit reached and the lab is quiet. Bay ramps to 37 C over 1 h. Pump fills all 24 wells with HEPES-buffered growth medium. Optical density read every 15 min.

  3. day 2

    Cultures reach stationary phase (expected 18 to 24 h), then are held without fresh medium so they starve, as EcAMSat did for over 24 h before dosing. Microscope takes a brightfield image of every well to check for aggregates before dosing.

  4. day 3

    Dosing. Gentamicin injected at 0, 25, 55 and 100 micrograms/mL, three wells per strain per level, then alamarBlue redox dye into all 24 wells. Optical readout at 570 and 600 nm every 30 min starts here and runs 72 h. Live/dead fluorescence images every 6 h.

  5. day 4

    Dose-response window continues. Downlink the optical series once per orbit so the team can confirm the assay is working while there is still time to extend the window.

  6. day 5

    Robotic arm carries the bay to the central lab for one 40x fluorescence look at the highest-dose wells (aggregates, matrix, vesicle-scale structures). Arm motion window logged, since quasi-steady microgravity is lost to 1e-4 to 1e-3 g while it moves.

  7. day 6

    End of exposure. 12 wells get RNA stabiliser (transcriptome snapshot taken in orbit), 6 wells get glutaraldehyde fixative for electron microscopy, 6 wells are left live for ground plating. Bay drops to 4 C and holds. Waste line chemically inactivated.

  8. day 7

    Deorbit, re-entry under the inflatable heat shield, splashdown. Bay stays at 4 C through recovery; samples handed to the customer courier on the recovery ship about 20 min after splashdown.

Measured in orbit

  • Metabolic activity / dose response alamarBlue redox dye reduction, read as 570 nm vs 600 nm absorbance by the per-well LED and photodiode pair every 30 min for 72 h. Same readout class EcAMSat flew, where metabolic activity was measured optically using the payload's LED and detector system.
  • Growth curve to stationary phase Optical density on the same LED/photodiode pair every 15 min from medium fill to dosing.
  • Live/dead fraction Bay microscope, two fluorescence channels, SYTO 9 and propidium iodide, imaged every 6 h after dosing.
  • Aggregation and matrix formation Brightfield images of every well before dosing and every 6 h after; one 40x fluorescence session in the central lab on day 5.
  • Environment and containment Bay temperature, pressure, humidity and seal sensors at 1 min cadence; accelerometer for the arm-motion windows.
  • Radiation dose Active silicon dosimeter logged through the flight, passive TLDs read after return.

Measured after the return

  • Viable counts CFU plating of the 6 live wells on arrival at the customer lab, plated against the ground control run.
  • MIC and MBC confirmation Standard broth microdilution on returned isolates, so the flight dye signal is anchored to a clinical susceptibility number.
  • Transcriptome RNA-seq on the 12 wells stabilised in orbit on day 6, compared with the ground control; targeted at the sigma-s (rpoS) and Hfq regulons.
  • Proteome LC-MS/MS on the stabilised lysates.
  • Cell envelope and ultrastructure Transmission electron microscopy on the glutaraldehyde-fixed wells, measuring envelope thickness, cell volume and outer membrane vesicles, as in the ISS E. coli work.
  • Genome check Whole-genome sequencing of returned isolates to show the change was not a selected mutation.
  • Virulence (optional, customer facility) Murine infection model on returned live cells, as done for the STS-115 Salmonella. Needs the customer's animal facility and ethics approval; not part of the standard service.

Ground control

An identical cassette in an identical bay inside the flight-spare satellite on the ground, run on a 24 h delayed timeline that replays the actual flight telemetry: measured bay temperature, pressure, humidity and the real fluidics command log, same cell lot, same cassette fill batch, same reagent lots. EcAMSat ran a parallel ground-based control experiment against its flight data, and a second ground-control experiment that showed multi-week storage in the payload hardware stressed the cells - so our ground arm also carries a storage-matched control, and the flight plan keeps load-to-dose time short. Because launch loads, re-entry loads and the 550 km radiation dose are not reproduced on the ground, we also fly a 1 g reference: three wells of each strain in a bay on the onboard centrifuge, so microgravity can be separated from the rest of the flight environment. Without that 1 g arm, any flight-versus-ground difference stays confounded.

Coming home

Fixed and RNA-stabilised wells are robust: 4 C and up to 48 h from day-6 preservation to the lab is fine. The live wells are the tight ones - plate them within 24 h of splashdown, because cells sitting at 4 C in spent medium keep changing expression and losing viability. That is why the transcriptome and electron microscopy samples are preserved in orbit on day 6 rather than on the ship: the only clean snapshot of the flight state is one taken in flight.

Limits of this flight

  • The flight evidence does not point one way. ISS E. coli grew to a 13-fold higher final cell count in space, while EcAMSat found uropathogenic E. coli more susceptible to gentamicin in microgravity and its growth rate unchanged. One 7-day flight will not settle that; it adds one well-controlled point.
  • A 2 to 14 day flight gives one dose-response, not evolution. Resistance that arises by selection over weeks of serial passage cannot be studied here.
  • EcAMSat incubated its cells with the dye for 144 h after dosing. Our 7-day baseline gives a 72 h window; matching EcAMSat's readout length needs a 10-day mission, which is inside the 2 to 14 day envelope but costs more.
  • No crew. There is no plating, no colony picking, no re-dosing decision in the moment and no fixing a stuck valve. Every move must be in the fluidic plan before launch, and a single pump or valve failure can cost the whole bay.
  • 24 wells means 3 replicates per condition. That resolves a large shift in tolerance, not a small one. EcAMSat's own report notes that cell stress in the hardware could obscure small differential effects, so customers wanting statistical power should buy more than one bay.
  • The in-flight readout is dye reduction, that is metabolic activity, not colony-forming units. A clinical MIC number must be confirmed on the ground, and returned live cells spend 1 to 2 days at 4 C before plating.
  • Only wells preserved in orbit give a clean flight-state transcriptome or ultrastructure. Anything judged after the courier hand-off has a day or two of cold storage written into it.
  • Launch loads, re-entry loads and the 550 km radiation dose are confounders. Only the onboard 1 g control separates microgravity from them, and that control shares the same radiation and vibration history, not the same gravity history as a laboratory on Earth.
  • Quasi-steady microgravity is about 1e-6 g near the centre of mass, but rises to 1e-4 to 1e-3 g while the robotic arm moves. Data taken in those windows is not a clean microgravity measurement and is flagged as such.
  • No animal virulence test on board. The STS-115 result that flight Salmonella killed mice faster needed returned live cells and a ground animal facility; LELP-1 can return the cells, nothing more.
  • No host. There is no infection model, no human pharmacokinetics and no immune system in the bay, so nothing here translates directly into a dosing recommendation for a crew member.
  • No CO2 supply. Fine for E. coli, but a bacteria-plus-host-cell co-culture would need HEPES-buffered media and gas-permeable lids and is a harder sell in a sealed bay.
  • LELP-1 is a design in a digital twin. No bay, cassette or thermoelectric block described here has flown, and every mass, power and timing number above is our estimate awaiting test.

A satellite of its own: SP-AMR B · samples return, hardware burns up

SP-AMR is a 117 kg free-flyer. Only the samples come home: 2.2 kg ride in a 45 kg capsule in a drawer behind an optical window; the microscope looks through the window from the service module, and fluid lines cross through dry-break couplings that part at separation once the last fluid step is done. The microscope, pumps and reservoirs stay in the service module and burn up with it. Why B: the in-flight metabolic readout flies alone on an EcAMSat-class CubeSat (option A), but plating, MIC, RNA-seq, proteome, TEM and genome checks need the cassette back.

Tailored for this experiment

  • Per-well absorbance photometry: 570 nm and 600 nm LED plus photodiode pairs on all 24 wells. The standard bay gives a microscope, not a plate reader, and the redox dye assay is the primary in-flight measurement.
  • Live/dead fluorescence filter set (SYTO 9 and propidium iodide) on the bay microscope.
  • A 1 g reference position on the onboard centrifuge for 3 wells of each strain, with the same thermal and fluidic service as the microgravity bay.
  • Extra reservoirs and lines for RNA stabiliser and glutaraldehyde, and the ability to drop the bay from 37 C to 4 C and hold it there from day 6 through recovery.
  • Bio-waste bag with chemical inactivation before splashdown, plus a verified seal log for a pathogenic strain under BSL-2 triple containment.

Bay or satellite?

The capsule is the floor: no capsule much smaller than about 45 kg has been recovered yet, so 2.2 kg of samples need 117 kg of satellite. On LELP-1 the same samples share one capsule, the whole lab, with 31 other bays. A dedicated satellite earns its place when the experiment needs the schedule to itself, a quieter ride (about 7.3 × 10⁻⁸ g quasi-steady, no robotic arm), or power a bay cannot give.

Option A, no return. The in-flight readout alone can fly on a CubeSat with no capsule: EcAMSat, NASA Ames' 6U biological CubeSat (10.7 kg), flew this assay in 2017; a 6U may weigh up to 12 kg. It keeps the in-orbit science and loses every post-flight assay.

First-order sizing with a 20 % margin by one model for every experiment, reviewed independently; caveats and sources. The smallest recovered capsule so far (Varda W-1) is under 90 kg, so treat these masses as optimistic.

Asks of the satellite

  • Per-well absorbance photometry: 570 nm and 600 nm LED plus photodiode pairs on all 24 wells. The standard bay gives a microscope, not a plate reader, and the redox dye assay is the primary in-flight measurement.
  • Live/dead fluorescence filter set (SYTO 9 and propidium iodide) on the bay microscope.
  • A 1 g reference position on the onboard centrifuge for 3 wells of each strain, with the same thermal and fluidic service as the microgravity bay.
  • Extra reservoirs and lines for RNA stabiliser and glutaraldehyde, and the ability to drop the bay from 37 C to 4 C and hold it there from day 6 through recovery.
  • Bio-waste bag with chemical inactivation before splashdown, plus a verified seal log for a pathogenic strain under BSL-2 triple containment.
  • Downlink of the optical series at least once per orbit during the 72 h antibiotic window, so the assay can be confirmed while the mission can still be extended.
  • Launch-site load of live stationary-phase cells within 24 h of liftoff and a 4 C hold on the pad; this experiment does not use the -80 C cryo path.
  • A short, controlled load-to-dose interval. EcAMSat found that multi-week storage in the payload hardware stressed the cells and may have hidden small differences, so late load, a short pad hold and early dosing are requirements, not conveniences.
