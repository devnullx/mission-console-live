Life support / bioproduction

Microalgae and cyanobacteria for oxygen and food

Long crewed missions cannot carry all their oxygen and food from Earth. Photosynthetic microbes such as the cyanobacterium Limnospira indica (spirulina) and the green alga Chlorella take up carbon dioxide, release oxygen and make edible, protein-rich biomass. Engineers need flight data on growth rate, oxygen output, purity and gene or protein changes before they size a bioreactor for a habitat. In microgravity there is no buoyancy-driven mixing and gas bubbles do not rise, so a flight test shows whether a reactor design still delivers light, nutrients and gas transfer the way the models predict.

Who it is for

  • Space-agency life support programmes that study bioregenerative systems, such as ESA's MELiSSA project
  • University and institute groups that study algal and cyanobacterial physiology in space (for example the ICGEB New Delhi groups that flew experiments on Axiom-4)
  • Companies building photobioreactors or algal food and feed products that want flight data on their hardware or strain
  • Groups testing nutrient recycling from waste streams, such as urea as a nitrogen source

How LELP-1 runs it

Sample. Limnospira indica PCC 8005 (axenic culture, the MELiSSA strain). A Chlorella strain can be flown instead in the same hardware. Container. Four flat-panel membrane photobioreactors (about 40 ml liquid each, 15 mm deep). Each has a liquid chamber and a gas headspace separated by a hydrophobic PTFE membrane. They sit in a large-bay cassette with an LED panel, a recirculation pump, and optical-density and oxygen sensors. Launch. Live culture at low density in bicarbonate-rich medium, kept dark at 10 C to slow growth until activation (our design choice; ground tests must confirm the culture survives the hold). Light and gas. Red/white LED light at about 35 to 80 umol photons m-2 s-1, set per reactor (our choice). Carbon comes from bicarbonate in the medium because the sealed bay has no CO2 supply. Oxygen collects in each reactor's headspace and is measured by pressure and an optical O2 sensor. Each headspace has a pressure relief; Arthrospira-B used 1.15 bar.

  1. day 0

    Launch with reactors filled, lights off, bay held at 10 C. Log temperature, acceleration and dose.

  2. day 1

    After orbit checkout, warm to 33 C, start recirculation mixing and switch on the LEDs. Reactors 1-2 at 35 umol photons m-2 s-1, reactors 3-4 at 70 umol photons m-2 s-1.

  3. day 1

    Start logging optical density every 30 min, and headspace pressure and O2 every 5 min. Take a baseline microscope image of each reactor's sampling window.

  4. day 4

    Pump 2 ml from each reactor into fixative wells (for RNA/protein) and image trichome shape and chlorophyll autofluorescence.

  5. day 7

    Repeat sampling and imaging. If a headspace nears its pressure limit, vent it through the bay's relief path and log the vented volume.

  6. day 7

    Replace 20 ml of culture in reactors 3-4 with fresh medium from the reservoir (semi-continuous mode) to test growth after dilution and bicarbonate top-up.

  7. day 10

    Final sampling into fixative wells and final imaging.

  8. day 11

    Switch off the LEDs, stop mixing, cool the bay to 4 C. Live culture stays in the reactors for viability and purity tests on the ground.

  9. day 12

    Hold at 4 C through deorbit, splashdown and handover to the customer's courier.

Measured in orbit

  • Growth (biomass) Optical density at about 750 nm, measured by LED and photodiode across each flat reactor and logged every 30 min
  • Oxygen production rate Headspace pressure rise in each reactor (the Arthrospira-B approach), cross-checked with an optical O2 sensor spot
  • Cell shape and pigment The bay's LumaScope-class microscope: brightfield for trichome length and coiling, and a fluorescence channel for chlorophyll and phycocyanin autofluorescence
  • Culture conditions Bay temperature, light (LED current and a PAR photodiode), acceleration log and active dosimeter dose rate

Measured after the return

  • Dry weight and productivity Filter and dry the remaining culture; compare with the in-flight OD curve
  • Pigments and protein Chlorophyll, carotenoid and phycocyanin by spectrophotometry; total protein content
  • Purity Plating and 16S rRNA sequencing to detect contaminants
  • pH and bicarbonate pH and dissolved inorganic carbon of the returned medium, to explain any late-batch slowdown
  • Gene and protein expression Transcriptomics and proteomics on fixed samples from days 4, 7 and 10
  • Viability Regrowth of the live returned culture under standard ground conditions
  • Passive dose Read-out of the bay TLDs

Ground control

An identical flight-model module runs on the ground at the same time. It uses the same culture lot, medium batch, light settings, temperature profile (replayed from flight telemetry, including the 10 C launch hold and 4 C return) and sampling times. Optional: a second set of reactors on a 1 g centrifuge in flight would separate microgravity effects from launch and radiation effects.

Coming home

Fixed samples and live culture both stay at 4 C and in the dark. Cool-down is on day 11, splashdown about a day later, and the courier needs 1-2 days, so plan for up to about 96 h from cool-down to the lab. The live culture is only for viability and purity; fixed samples tolerate longer. This is our estimate and must be confirmed by the customer's ground tests.

Limits of this flight

  • The sealed bay has no CO2 supply, so growth is batch or semi-continuous on bicarbonate in the medium. It cannot copy a reactor fed by cabin CO2 the way PBR@LSR was. Arthrospira-B saw oxygen output drop late in each batch, which its authors linked to bicarbonate limitation.
  • 12 days and about 160 ml of culture cannot test the months-long stability a real life support reactor needs. PBR@LSR was designed for up to 180 days.
  • No crew: bubbles, membrane fouling or a stuck pump cannot be fixed by hand. Running four reactors in parallel is the only backup.
  • No pH probe or pH control in this design (Arthrospira-B had none either), so pH drift is only measured after return.
  • There is too little biomass to test taste, food safety or feeding. Only composition and purity can be checked.
  • The live culture must survive a dark hold at launch and several days of cold return. This may bias viability and gene expression results.
  • Radiation in a 550 km sun-synchronous orbit differs from the ISS, so dose results do not compare directly with ISS data.
  • Robotic-arm moves raise acceleration to about 1e-4 to 1e-3 g. Arm activity should pause during key measurements.

A satellite of its own: SP-ALG B · samples return, hardware burns up

SP-ALG is a 115 kg free-flyer. Only the samples come home: 2.8 kg ride in a 45 kg capsule in a drawer behind an optical window; the microscope looks through the window from the service module, and fluid lines cross through dry-break couplings that part at separation once the last fluid step is done. The microscope, pumps and reservoirs stay in the service module and burn up with it. Why B: the live culture and the fixed samples from days 4, 7 and 10 come back cold and dark.

Tailored for this experiment

  • Controlled LED lighting inside the bay with a light sensor (the standard bay has no growth light)
  • Continuous mixing by recirculation, because there is no buoyancy-driven mixing or bubble rise
  • Optical density and oxygen sensing for each reactor
  • A headspace pressure relief or vent path inside the triple containment for the oxygen produced
  • Optional 1 g centrifuge position for an in-flight control

Bay or satellite?

The capsule is the floor: no capsule much smaller than about 45 kg has been recovered yet, so 2.8 kg of samples need 115 kg of satellite. On LELP-1 the same samples share one capsule, the whole lab, with 31 other bays. A dedicated satellite earns its place when the experiment needs the schedule to itself, a quieter ride (about 7.3 × 10⁻⁸ g quasi-steady, no robotic arm), or power a bay cannot give.

First-order sizing with a 20 % margin by one model for every experiment, reviewed independently; caveats and sources. The smallest recovered capsule so far (Varda W-1) is under 90 kg, so treat these masses as optimistic.
