Muscle biology / ageing

C. elegans muscle and ageing

Astronauts lose muscle in orbit, and people on Earth lose muscle with age, disuse and diseases such as Duchenne muscular dystrophy. The worm C. elegans grows from larva to adult in about four days, has well-mapped genetics, and has been studied in space for more than 20 years. On ISS flights, worms grown in microgravity had less myosin heavy chain, smaller body-wall muscle cells and less strength, and similar gene changes appeared across missions. An uncrewed free-flyer could repeat these runs more often, without waiting for crew time, to test which genes and drugs protect muscle.

How LELP-1 runs it

Sample. C. elegans hermaphrodites: wild-type N2 plus one muscle-relevant strain chosen by the customer, for example dys-1 (BZ33), a body-wall muscle GFP reporter, or the Q35::YFP polyglutamine line. Each well holds about 50-100 age-synchronised L1 larvae. Container. 24-well gas-permeable culture cassette (about 0.5 mL liquid per well, thin gas-permeable film lid) inside the sealed bay. Two identical large modules fly: Module A (young-adult time point) and Module B (aged time point). Each bay block holds only one temperature, so the two time points need separate bays. Launch. Live, starved L1 larvae arrested in buffer (M9 with cholesterol) without food, held at 12 C. CERISE used a similar approach: L1 larvae held in stasis in buffer, then fed on orbit to start the experiment. Light and gas. Dark, except for short LED pulses during imaging. Air headspace through the gas-permeable lid in the sealed bay; no CO2 needed. Worm number and liquid volume are kept low because MME ground tests found gas exchange limits growth in sealed bags.

  1. day -2

    Ground: bleach gravid adults, hatch eggs overnight in buffer to get synchronised L1 larvae, load each well, seal the cassettes, close the triple containment. Load reservoirs: freeze-dried E. coli OP50 food, FUdR, paraformaldehyde fixative, RNA preservation solution. Hold at 12 C.

  2. day 0

    Launch. Both modules hold 12 C; larvae stay arrested without food.

  3. day 1

    After orbit checkout and once the arm is parked (quasi-steady microgravity), raise both modules to 20 C. Pump bacterial food into every well to start development. Take baseline brightfield images of each well.

  4. day 2

    Daily, both modules: 30-60 s brightfield video per well (swimming frequency, body length, stage); fluorescence images if a reporter strain flies. Downlink a compressed subset.

  5. day 3

    Worms near L4: add FUdR to both modules (NIS used 75 uM) to stop new offspring before egg laying starts. Top up food.

  6. day 5

    Module A (about day 1 of adulthood): final video and fluorescence imaging. Then fix 12 wells with paraformaldehyde (for muscle staining and body length) and fill 12 wells with RNA preservation solution (for gene expression). Drop Module A to 4 C and hold it there until the courier hands it over.

  7. day 7

    Module B: top up food and FUdR. Continue daily imaging. Repeat every 2-3 days.

  8. day 13

    Module B (about day 9 of adulthood): final video and fluorescence imaging. Fix 12 wells and preserve 12 wells for RNA, as in Module A. Drop Module B to 4 C.

  9. day 14

    Deorbit and splashdown with both modules at 4 C. Recovery ship hands the sealed modules to the customer's courier (about 20 min after splashdown); cold transport at 4 C to the lab (about 1-2 days).

Measured in orbit

  • Swimming (thrashing) frequency and wave shape Brightfield video from the bay's inverted microscope, tracked frame by frame. CERISE measured swimming from downlinked movies.
  • Body length and developmental stage Brightfield images per well, measured with image analysis. CERISE found body length about 5.5% shorter in microgravity.
  • Population and survival per well Count moving and still worms from video; check for contamination or starvation.
  • Muscle reporter fluorescence or polyglutamine aggregates Bay fluorescence channel for a GFP or YFP muscle reporter. Aggregate counts and muscle cell detail need the 40x central microscope, reached by moving the bay with the arm.
  • Environment log Bay temperature, pressure, humidity and acceleration sensors; active silicon dosimeter and passive TLDs for radiation dose.

Measured after the return

  • Body-wall muscle cell area and muscle-to-body-length ratio Phalloidin staining of fixed worms and fluorescence microscopy, as in the MME and NIS analysis
  • Myosin heavy chain and other muscle protein levels Quantitative western blot or mass-spectrometry proteomics, as in ICE-First and CERISE
  • Gene expression (myogenic factors, mitochondrial, cytoskeletal, synaptic and longevity genes) RNA sequencing or qPCR on RNA-preserved wells, compared with the ground control
  • Young versus aged comparison Compare Module A (day 1 adult) with Module B (about day 9 adult), flight against ground, for muscle structure and transcripts
  • Polyglutamine aggregate count (if Q35::YFP flies) Fluorescence microscopy count of aggregates per worm, as in the ICE-First follow-up analysis
  • Radiation dose TLD readout to report the total dose the worms received

Ground control

Two identical ground modules with the same worm batches, food, reservoirs and cassette lots, run in a flight-like bay. Each step follows the flight timeline, with the bay's recorded temperature profile replayed about 1-2 days behind the flight (micro-16 replicated the flight temperature profile on a two-day delay). Ground modules are fixed, preserved, chilled and shipped the same way. This control cannot separate microgravity from space radiation or launch and re-entry loads. That needs an in-flight 1 G centrifuge control, as flown on CERISE and NIS.

Coming home

Fixed and RNA-preserved worms return sealed at 4 C. Module A samples are about 9 days past preservation at splashdown and about 10-11 days by the time they reach the lab. The 336 h (14 day) window is OUR ESTIMATE, and the customer must check it on the ground for both the fixative and the RNA solution. Heritage flights froze worms for gene and protein work: liquid nitrogen within 2 h of landing on ICE-First, and -80 C on orbit on CERISE, MME and micro-16. Only the NIS morphology samples were PFA-fixed and held at 4 C. If live worms are wanted for after-flight motility or lifespan tests, an extra well set must return unfixed at 15-20 C and reach the lab within about 48 h; this is also our estimate.

Limits of this flight

  • 14 days of lab time covers development and early ageing (to about day 9 of adulthood) only; the worms' full lifespan cannot be followed in orbit
  • No on-orbit freezing to -80 C. All the heritage gene and protein datasets we checked came from frozen worms, so chemical preservation (RNA solution, paraformaldehyde) at 4 C is a change that must be validated on the ground
  • No crew, so the micro-16 strength assay (crew loaded worms into micropillar devices) cannot be repeated as flown; strength would need a new automated chip
  • Without an in-flight 1 G centrifuge, the experiment cannot separate microgravity from radiation and launch or re-entry effects; CERISE and NIS had this control
  • Sealed wells limit oxygen and food, so worm number per well must stay low; MME ground tests found gas exchange limited growth in sealed bags, and a lid-only well has less gas-exchange area per mL than the heritage bags
  • The bay microscope is low magnification; sarcomere and mitochondrial detail needs the 40x central microscope or post-flight staining
  • Arm moves raise acceleration to about 1e-4 to 1e-3 g, which may disturb swimming measurements; imaging must avoid those periods
  • About 2 h to hand-over and 1-2 days to the lab means live worms partly readapt to 1 g before any post-flight motility test
  • Worm muscle has no satellite stem cells and differs from human muscle; results point to mechanisms and need follow-up in mammalian models
  • Two L bays per run use a large share of the 16 large bays

A satellite of its own: SP-WORM B · samples return, hardware burns up

SP-WORM is a 137 kg free-flyer. Only the samples come home: 2.3 kg ride in a 45 kg capsule in a drawer behind an optical window; the microscope looks through the window from the service module, and fluid lines cross through dry-break couplings that part at separation once the last fluid step is done. The microscope, pumps and reservoirs stay in the service module and burn up with it. Why B: two modules fly (young and aged); both cassettes come home, fixed or RNA-preserved.

Tailored for this experiment

  • Two large bays at once (one per time point), because each bay block holds a single temperature setpoint
  • Launch hold at 12 C from handover until orbit, then a step to 20 C, then 4 C after preservation and through re-entry and recovery (all inside the 4-45 C range, but the bay must be powered during ascent and descent)
  • Video imaging: 30-60 s brightfield clips per well each day, with some downlink bandwidth for compressed video
  • Arm transfer of each module to the central 40x fluorescence microscope for muscle reporter and aggregate imaging, scheduled so arm moves do not overlap the daily swimming videos
  • Optional: a small 1 G centrifuge position for an in-flight gravity control (not part of the standard bay)

Bay or satellite?

The capsule is the floor: no capsule much smaller than about 45 kg has been recovered yet, so 2.3 kg of samples need 137 kg of satellite. On LELP-1 the same samples share one capsule, the whole lab, with 31 other bays. A dedicated satellite earns its place when the experiment needs the schedule to itself, a quieter ride (about 7.3 × 10⁻⁸ g quasi-steady, no robotic arm), or power a bay cannot give.

First-order sizing with a 20 % margin by one model for every experiment, reviewed independently; caveats and sources. The smallest recovered capsule so far (Varda W-1) is under 90 kg, so treat these masses as optimistic.
