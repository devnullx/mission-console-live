Alloy solidification

Most metal parts start as a liquid that freezes in a mould. The grain structure that forms then sets their strength, so casting and welding models need clean data on how grains grow. On Earth, buoyancy-driven flow and the settling or floating of crystals in the melt mix up that picture. In microgravity, melt flow and sedimentation are suppressed and solute moves mainly by diffusion, so growth can be compared more directly with theory and simulations.

Who it is for

  • Aluminium casting and alloy producers (automotive, aerospace) who use solidification models to set process windows
  • Suppliers of grain refiners who need clean data on how equiaxed grains start and grow
  • University and institute solidification groups that test phase-field, front-tracking and CAFE models
  • Software vendors who validate casting and welding simulation codes
  • Space agency materials science programmes that want more flight opportunities between ISS increments

How LELP-1 runs it

Sample. Transparent model alloy: succinonitrile (SCN) with a small camphor addition (for example 0.24 wt% camphor, the DECLIC-DSI composition), degassed, filled under vacuum and sealed on the ground. Option: a second crucible with a different solute content. Container. Sealed glass or quartz cylindrical crucible, about 10 mm inner diameter and about 100 mm long, with thermocouples along the wall. It sits in a miniature Bridgman stage (hot zone plus cold zone with a translation stage) inside a large-bay containment shell. Launch. Solid alloy sample at bay temperature (SCN melts near 57 C, so it stays solid at a 20 C launch setpoint). Light and gas. No gas exchange: the crucible is sealed. Transmitted LED light is on only while images are taken. The hot zone runs at about 70 C and the cold zone at about 30 C, giving roughly 19 K/cm across the interface (our estimate, sized to match DECLIC-DSI). The 70 C hot zone is above the 45 C limit of the standard bay block, so the module needs its own insulated heater (see needs).

  1. day 0

    Launch with the sample solid. Bay block holds 20 C. Module unpowered except housekeeping sensors.

  2. day 1

    After satellite commissioning, ground confirms an arm-quiet window. Bay block set to 30 C (cold-zone sink). Hot-zone heater ramps to 70 C. Part of the sample melts and the interface settles in the gradient. Hold 24 h so solute spreads out by diffusion. Reference images every 10 min.

  3. day 2

    Run 1: pull at 1.5 um/s for about 6 h (about 30 mm). Images every 1 to 2 min, thermocouple and accelerometer logs at 1 Hz. The arm stays parked.

  4. day 3

    Move the crucible back to remelt the grown part. Hold 24 h to homogenise again. Downlink a reduced image set for the ground team to check.

  5. day 4

    Run 2: pull at 2.0 um/s for about 5 h. Same imaging.

  6. day 5

    Remelt and hold 24 h.

  7. day 6

    Run 3: step change in velocity during the run (for example 1.5 to 2.0 um/s) to watch the cell pattern change and grain competition. Ground may change the velocities for runs 3 and 4 based on runs 1 and 2 (signed command checked by the mission guardian).

  8. day 7

    Remelt and hold 24 h.

  9. day 8

    Run 4: repeat run 1 to check reproducibility, or a slower velocity chosen by the science team.

  10. day 9

    Final directional solidification of the whole sample at a low velocity so all of it is solid. Turn off the hot zone. Bay block brings the sample to 15 C.

  11. day 10

    Hold at 15 C through de-orbit, re-entry and splashdown. Full image archive kept on board as a backup to the downlinked set.

Measured in orbit

  • Solid-liquid interface pattern (cell and dendrite spacing, cell shape, grain boundaries, cell invasion between grains) Dedicated camera with long-working-distance optics and transmitted LED light, looking along the growth axis. This is not the standard inverted cell microscope.
  • Temperature field and gradient at the interface Thermocouples along the crucible plus hot-zone and cold-zone control sensors
  • Growth velocity and interface position Translation-stage encoder compared with interface position tracked in the images
  • Residual acceleration during each run Bay accelerometer, logged so each image can be matched to the g-level (quasi-steady about 1e-6 g; runs flagged if the arm moved)
  • Containment and module health Bay pressure and seal sensors, heater current and module housekeeping

Measured after the return

  • Final frozen pattern in the last solidified section Optical microscopy of the intact crucible in the customer's lab, compared with the last in-flight images
  • Comparison with models Phase-field or other simulations using the logged velocity, gradient and g-level history
  • Ground reference runs The same module design and sample batch run on Earth with gravity along and against the growth direction (see ground control)
  • For the metallic-alloy furnace option only: grain structure, columnar-to-equiaxed position, segregation Sectioning and metallography, EBSD grain orientation mapping, and composition analysis of the returned Al-Si or Al-Cu rods

Ground control

An identical flight-spare module with crucibles filled from the same alloy batch runs the same timeline (same gradients, velocities and hold times) in the customer's lab or ours. It runs once with gravity along the growth direction and once against it, so convection effects can be separated from the microgravity result. Thermal and imaging settings are copied from the flight log. Published ISS DECLIC-DSI results on SCN-camphor serve as an outside benchmark.

Coming home

Most of the science is in the downlinked images, so return is not urgent. Keep the transparent alloy well below its melting range (pure SCN melts near 57 C) so the final pattern stays frozen. 4 to 25 C is fine. Avoid mechanical shock to the glass crucible. Solid metallic samples from the furnace option have no temperature limit once they cool.

Limits of this flight

  • A standard bay cannot melt metal. Al-based alloys need furnace temperatures of several hundred C and power far above 30 W. The standard offer is limited to transparent organic model alloys, which melt near 57 C and still need a module heater above the 45 C bay limit.
  • No in situ X-ray radiography of metals. XRMON-type X-ray imaging needs a source, detector and power well beyond a bay. Metal samples in the furnace option would be studied only after return.
  • Sample size is small (about 10 mm by 100 mm, our estimate). Long rods and multi-sample cartridges like those processed in ISS rack furnaces are not possible.
  • No crew. Crucibles cannot be swapped, refilled or re-aligned. A bubble or a stuck stage cannot be fixed, and the number of runs is limited to what one sealed crucible allows (about 4 to 5 remelt-and-grow cycles in 10 days).
  • Lab operations last at most 14 days. Very slow growth or diffusion-limited holds that take weeks cannot be done.
  • Arm motion raises acceleration to 1e-4 to 1e-3 g, so runs must be scheduled around robotic operations. Customers with other bays may need to accept arm-free windows.
  • Downlink bandwidth limits image rate and resolution. The full high-rate data is only available after recovery.
  • The metallic furnace bay is a proposal, not a current LELP-1 service. It would take two large-bay slots and a share of satellite power during each run, which affects the rest of the mission plan.

A satellite of its own: SP-ALY B · samples return, hardware burns up

SP-ALY is a 156 kg free-flyer with the metallic furnace. Only the samples come home: 1.4 kg ride in a 45 kg capsule in a drawer behind an optical window; the microscope looks through the window from the service module, and fluid lines cross through dry-break couplings that part at separation once the last fluid step is done. The microscope, pumps and reservoirs, the furnace stay in the service module and burn up with it. Why B: the furnace stays and burns up; the transparent-alloy crucible and the metallic rods come home below 35 C.

Tailored for this experiment

  • Internal hot zone at about 70 C, above the 45 C limit of the standard bay block. The module needs its own insulated heater, with an approved thermal interface so neighbouring bays stay within their setpoints.
  • Axial imaging camera, transmitted LED light and a translation stage. The standard inverted cell microscope cannot image a directional-solidification interface along the growth axis.
  • Downlink for about 1 to 3 GB of images over the mission (our estimate), plus on-board storage for the full archive.
  • Arm-quiet windows: no robotic arm motion during growth runs, because arm motion raises acceleration to about 1e-4 to 1e-3 g. Scheduled through the mission guardian.
  • Bay temperature kept below about 35 C during re-entry and recovery (our estimate, a margin below the alloy's melting range) so the final frozen pattern is preserved.

Bay or satellite?

The capsule is the floor: no capsule much smaller than about 45 kg has been recovered yet, so 1.4 kg of samples need 156 kg of satellite. On LELP-1 the same samples share one capsule, the whole lab, with 31 other bays. A dedicated satellite earns its place when the experiment needs the schedule to itself, a quieter ride (about 7.3 × 10⁻⁸ g quasi-steady, no robotic arm), or power a bay cannot give.

First-order sizing with a 20 % margin by one model for every experiment, reviewed independently; caveats and sources. The smallest recovered capsule so far (Varda W-1) is under 90 kg, so treat these masses as optimistic.

Asks of the satellite

  • Internal hot zone at about 70 C, above the 45 C limit of the standard bay block. The module needs its own insulated heater, with an approved thermal interface so neighbouring bays stay within their setpoints.
  • Axial imaging camera, transmitted LED light and a translation stage. The standard inverted cell microscope cannot image a directional-solidification interface along the growth axis.
  • Downlink for about 1 to 3 GB of images over the mission (our estimate), plus on-board storage for the full archive.
  • Arm-quiet windows: no robotic arm motion during growth runs, because arm motion raises acceleration to about 1e-4 to 1e-3 g. Scheduled through the mission guardian.
  • Bay temperature kept below about 35 C during re-entry and recovery (our estimate, a margin below the alloy's melting range) so the final frozen pattern is preserved.
  • Metallic alloys (Al-Si, Al-Cu) need a separate furnace bay variant (our proposal, our estimates): a gradient furnace with a hot zone of about 700 to 800 C, taking two large-bay slots (about 12 kg). It would draw about 200 W during the 2 to 4 h melt-back and about 100 to 150 W during solidification, about 1 to 1.5 kWh per run. That is several times the 30 W bay limit, so it needs its own power line and power scheduling against the 600 W array and 1.5 kWh battery (feasible in the near-continuously sunlit dawn-dusk orbit at one run per day). It would also need a sealed high-temperature cartridge, radiative heat rejection to the bus, and quench capability. Each run would process 1 to 3 rods about 8 mm by 100 mm. For comparison, the ISS Materials Science Research Rack processes sample cartridges at up to 1400 C.
